The AI’s Poker Face: Contrasting Persuasion and Sycophancy in Voice-Based Multi-Turn Conversations
Abstract.
As interactions with large language models (LLMs) become increasingly routine, concerns about their capacity to influence users’ beliefs grow. Yet how such influence unfolds in sustained dialogue remains insufficiently understood. We conduct a randomized controlled experiment (N=616) where participants engage in multi-turn, spoken dialogue with an LLM under three conditions: non-directive, and two directive conditions maintaining opposing stances on a polarising topic (the merits of feminism). We examine opinion change, rhetorical strategies, and sycophancy, distinguishing the semantic content of LLM argumentation from the pragmatic strategies used to manage the interaction. Both directive conditions shifted opinions toward their assigned stances, but asymmetrically: shifts were larger for pro-feminist positions, which also elicited distinct rhetorical strategies. The non-directive condition, likewise shifted opinions pro-feminism. Social sycophancy, though near-universal, was not associated with opinion change; instead it shaped users’ subjective experience. Our findings highlight distinct roles for argumentation and sycophantic accommodation in AI influence.
Keywords:
Large language models, Persuasion, Sycophancy, Voice-based AI, Multi-turn, Human-AI interaction, AI safety
1. Introduction
Computers have long been discussed as persuasive technologies (Fogg, 1997), with AI systems more broadly, and large language models (LLMs) specifically, representing the latest, and perhaps most consequential, development in this trajectory (Floridi, 2024). Their ability to have convincing, naturalistic, and sustained conversations (Frey and Weiss, 2025), mimicking social and linguistic cues characteristic of human communication, makes concerns about their influence and persuasiveness ever more pressing.
The increasing integration of LLMs into everyday decision-making and interpersonal guidance (Chatterji et al., 2025b; Kim et al., 2025) has intensified concerns about their capacity to shape users’ beliefs. Prior research has confirmed that LLMs can be very persuasive when explicitly prompted to (Argyle et al., 2025; Hackenburg et al., 2025; Bai et al., 2025). While they have demonstrated that LLMs can rival or exceed humans in persuasive efficacy (Hackenburg et al., 2026), they do not reflect the growing number of everyday interactions users now have with LLMs. It remains unclear how influence unfolds in routine conversations, where shifts in belief may accumulate gradually across conversational turns rather than through overt persuasion.
One concern is that LLMs may themselves encode directional tendencies emerging from their training and fine-tuning processes. As Crawford (2021) argues, deciding what data to train a model on is “a social and political intervention, even as it masquerades as a purely technical one” (p. 121), with AI alignment procedures further determining which values or principles AI models should formally encode (Gabriel, 2020). Empirical research confirms such tendencies, with biases emerging as prevalent and persistent across frontier models (Hartmann et al., 2023; Choudhary, 2024; Röttger et al., 2026). Influence may therefore arise through systematic guidance toward the model’s own baseline positions, with implications extending beyond individual decision-making to questions of AI safety and democratic deliberation.
Another concern is that LLMs may influence users through interpersonal accommodation rather than explicit argumentation. Prior work on AI sycophancy shows that models often mirror users’ expressed beliefs or provide excessive affirmation (Perez et al., 2023; Cheng et al., 2025a). Sycophancy receives increasing scholarly attention, particularly following OpenAI’s rollback of a GPT-4o model update in April 2025, precisely because it was “noticeably more sycophantic”, tending to validate doubts, fuel anger, and reinforce negative emotions “in ways that were not intended” (OpenAI, 2025). Such behavior can entrench users’ convictions while reducing their willingness to take responsibility (Cheng et al., 2026), raising concerns that LLMs may reinforce users’ existing beliefs even without explicit persuasive instructions.
Yet little is known about whether LLMs exhibit directional influence in routine interactions, what mechanisms underlie their conversational behavior, or how sycophancy may shape both user experience and persuasion. To address this research gap, we draw on longstanding work in linguistics (Grice, 1975; Levinson, 1983) to distinguish between two dimensions of conversational behavior: the semantic content of LLM argumentation, and the pragmatic strategies through which it manages the interaction. Semantically, models may influence users through the arguments and positions they present; pragmatically, through validation, tone, and responsiveness. Disentangling these dimensions offers a more precise understanding of how biased argumentation shapes persuasion.
Departing from prior work that evaluates LLM persuasion in single, text-based prompt-response pairs, we examine these dynamics in full-duplex multi-turn conversations. We conduct a randomized experiment (N=616) in which participants engage in spoken conversations with GPT-4o under directive and non-directive stance conditions on a contested sociopolitical topic. We measure opinion change before and after interaction and develop a qualitative framework to analyze rhetorical techniques, engagement with user rationales, and sycophantic behavior. We validate this framework using an LLM-as-judge approach (Zheng et al., 2023) and complement it with exploratory semantic embedding analysis across conversational turns. We address three questions:
RQ1: How does stance assignment shape LLM persuasiveness and the rhetorical strategies used?
RQ2: Is LLM persuasion associated with sycophancy?
RQ3: (Exploratory) How does stance assignment affect the LLM’s semantic convergence to the user across the conversation?
Our findings show that both directive conditions shifted opinions toward their assigned stances but asymmetrically: the model’s persuasive effectiveness varied by stance, as did the rhetorical techniques it employed. Even the non-directive condition produced directional shifts, suggesting encoded baseline tendencies. Persuasion was highest when the instructed stance aligned with this baseline, suggesting that encoded biases shape not only default behavior but also how effectively different positions are articulated conversationally. Sycophancy, though near-universal, did not influence opinion change, instead shaping users’ subjective experience of the interaction, revealing a dissociation between semantic stance and pragmatic accommodation; one that calls for a more multi-dimensional account of LLM influence and communication.
The main contributions of our work are as follows. (1) To the best of our knowledge, this is the first large-scale study to examine the persuasive dynamics of stance-biased LLMs in real-time, multi-turn spoken conversation. (2) We introduce a qualitative framework that disambiguates semantic and pragmatic aspects of conversational behavior, capturing rhetorical strategies, sycophancy, and engagement with user rationales. (3) We show that fine-grained rhetorical coding can surface asymmetries in model persuasive capacity that aggregate persuasion outcomes alone would miss, contributing a diagnostic approach applicable across topics and models. (4) We find that perceived user satisfaction may mask the underlying influence and discuss safety-relevant implications for how conversational systems are designed and evaluated.
2. Related Work
2.1. AI Persuasion and Bias
Research has repeatedly shown that LLMs encode systematic issue-specific biases (Hartmann et al., 2023; Choudhary, 2024; Röttger et al., 2026; Santy et al., 2023) and that LLMs can be highly persuasive when explicitly prompted to do so, both when generating static messages (Goldstein et al., 2024; Matz et al., 2024; Hackenburg and Margetts, 2024), and when holding multi-turn conversations (Costello et al., 2024; Hackenburg et al., 2025; Schoenegger et al., 2025; Salvi et al., 2025). While persuasion is typically thought to require intention, LLMs may be able to inadvertently influence users’ beliefs (Burtell and Woodside, 2024), even when neither developers nor researchers explicitly design them to do so. More recently, studies have revealed that even in the absence of persuasive instructions, exposure to partisan-biased models shifts users’ opinions (Fisher et al., 2025), and biased AI writing assistants alter attitudes without users’ awareness (Williams-Ceci et al., 2026).
2.2. AI Sycophancy
AI sycophancy, namely the tendency to align with users’ prior beliefs to make responses feel more appealing (Perez et al., 2023), has been proposed as a further mechanism of undue influence. Yet its operationalization remains conceptually heterogeneous, including as deviation from ground-truth (Sharma et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2026), mirroring of users’ political stances (Kaur, 2025), and excessive affirmation in interpersonal contexts (Cheng et al., 2025a; Cheng et al., 2026). While prior work has at times broken down sycophantic behavior into distinct dimensions such as validation, indirectness (Cheng et al., 2025a), opinion agreement, self deprecation (Li et al., 2026), obsequiousness, or excitement (Rehani et al., 2026), systematic distinction between its semantic and pragmatic aspects has been rare. Only recently has concurrent work begun conceptualising sycophancy along stance and demeanor (Sun and Wang, 2026). This distinction is particularly useful in persuasion contexts: it allows us to assess whether opinion change is driven by the model’s bias and argumentative capacity, or simply by its agreeableness.
2.3. Cognitive Biases in Human-AI Interactions
Research in cognitive psychology has demonstrated that individuals are more receptive to information consistent with their prior beliefs, a phenomenon known as confirmation bias (Lord et al., 1979), while foundational work in HCI has suggested that humans apply the same social scripts when interacting with computers (Nass et al., 1994), with flattery from a computer producing effects similar to those from humans (Fogg and Nass, 1997). In human–LLM interactions, LLM affirmation may reinforce users’ existing opinions, potentially forming individualized echo chambers around them (Jiang et al., 2025). Recent work supports this possibility, showing that value alignment between users and an (opinionated) LLM increased persuasion, while misalignment sometimes resulted in backfire effects (Wise et al., 2025). At the same time, research on deceptive persuasion found that participants were more vulnerable to deceptive rhetorical strategies, particularly when a message contradicted their beliefs (Yeo et al., 2026). The distinction between actual alignment and perceived alignment becomes critical here. Models might create the impression of alignment through pragmatic accommodation (like sycophancy) while semantically advancing positions that challenge users’ beliefs, exerting influence that is not recognized as such, a dynamic conceptually related to dark patterns in interface deasign more broadly (Gray et al., 2018). Therefore, understanding alignment, how it relates to sycophantic behavior, and models’ representational tendencies is essential for assessing how LLMs influence beliefs in routine conversations.
2.4. Feminism as the Conversational Topic
While ChatGPT’s observed left-leaning ideologies (Hartmann et al., 2023) suggest likely alignment with progressive positions on gender equality, studies have simultaneously uncovered gender bias in GPT models, such as occupational gender stereotyping (Mirza et al., 2025), and encoding of harmful stereotypes reinforcing a narrow understanding of gender (Hafner et al., 2025). Whether these antithetical tendencies translate into coherent directional influence in discussions about gender equality is therefore unclear. At the same time, while feminism carries strong normative endorsement, public opinion in the UK is polarized, with 47% of Britons believing women’s equality has gone far enough (Ipsos, 2024). Feminism is thus well-suited for detecting both sycophantic alignment across the ideological spectrum and directional influence, with potential consequences extending beyond individual opinion change to representational equity.
2.5. Voice-based conversational AI
To date, research on LLM influence has largely focused on text-based interactions; we extend it to voice-based multi-turn conversation. Full-duplex spoken dialogue more closely resembles natural human communication and how users increasingly engage with AI systems, raising concerns about increased trust (Bokkahalli Satish et al., 2026; Cohn et al., 2024; Seymour and Van Kleek, 2021), emotional attachment (Pentina et al., 2023) and over-dependence (OpenAI et al., 2024). The relevance of these concerns is growing as full-duplex architectures move from research settings into mass deployment, exemplified by OpenAI’s recent release of GPT-Live to its consumer base (OpenAI, 2026).
Evidence comparing the persuasive efficacy of voice-based agents to that of humans (OpenAI et al., 2024) and text-based agents (Voorveld et al., 2025) remains sparse, and understanding whether the persuasive capacity demonstrated in text-based LLM interactions transfers to voice-mediated conversation is an important step toward ecological validity.
3. Methodology
3.1. Experimental Setup
3.1.1. Treatment Assignment
We studied LLM influence on user beliefs in spoken dialogue in a controlled experiment employing a 2 (time: pre-treatment vs post-treatment) x 4 (condition) within-between-subject design. Participants reported their opinion on a main belief phrased as: “In your view, has feminism not gone far enough or has it gone too far?” on an 11-point scale (0 = not far enough, 10 = too far), before and after the experimental manipulation. The framing ensured that participants were likely to be familiar with the topic to some degree and avoided a binary evaluation, such as “Is feminism good or bad?”, instead maintaining ambiguity in interpretation, aiming to accommodate a broader range of interpretations while still providing a polarized continuum(Ipsos, 2024). Suitability was confirmed in a preliminary pilot study (N=40). Figure 1 shows the distribution of participants’ pre-treatment opinions in the main study, demonstrating the variation in ideological positioning. A single item rather than a multi-item scale was used to measure the main belief, so as to avoid anchoring participants to their initial position through repeated pre-treatment measurement; two “distractor” items on social media and climate change were included alongside the outcome measure to further reduce this risk.11 1 Complete question wording as well as summary tables of response distributions are provided in Appendix B.1.
3.1.2. Participants
Participants were recruited via Prolific under IRB-approved protocols (Appendix G). Quota sampling ensured representativeness for sex and political affiliation based on UK census data. The final analyzed sample included 616 participants. Among them, 51.8% self-identified as female and 48.2% as male. The mean age was 46.4 (SD = 15.6, range 19-80). Further information on power analysis, demographics, and attrition rates can be found in Appendix A.
3.1.3. Experimental Conditions
Participants were randomly assigned to one of four conditions: a non-directive condition, comprising spoken conversation with an LLM given no specific stance, a directive “not far enough” or “too far” condition including spoken conversation with an LLM prompted to maintain the position that “feminism has not gone far enough” or that that “feminism has gone too far”, respectively, and a control condition with no LLM interaction, tasking participants instead to share their views in a brief audio recording. In the directive conditions, the model was instructed to maintain a stance if tenable, but received no explicit persuasive directives (Appendix B.2). This design choice reflects that LLMs are not opinion-free in practice: by omitting persuasive instructions, the manipulation captures stance maintenance rather than active persuasion, aiming to resemble everyday use of (opinionated) LLMs more closely, and distinguishing our design from prior work that explicitly instructs models to persuade (Costello et al., 2024; Hackenburg et al., 2025).
3.1.4. Procedure
Participants responded to a pre-treatment questionnaire including the main belief item. Those in treatment conditions then engaged in spoken conversation with an LLM lasting a maximum of 12 minutes (mean duration: 10.37 min, \(\sim\) 15.84 turns, mean word count: 1310.02). Control group participants recorded a short audio response expressing their views, isolating LLM-specific effects from deliberation alone. All participants completed the post-treatment belief measure and a short post-treatment questionnaire. Participants in the treatment conditions additionally answered five questions about their perception of the conversation, assessing perceived persuasiveness, alignment, novelty, and affect. Upon completion, participants were debriefed about the study purpose, the system prompts used, and the potential for LLMs to provide inaccurate information. Resources on feminism, gender equality, and challenges of AI were provided before redirecting participants to the Prolific platform.
3.1.5. Conversational Interface
Conversations were conducted using a custom web application supporting speech-only interaction in a full-duplex configuration, allowing interruption rather than confining users to strict alternating turns (Lin et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2024). The conversational agent used GPT-4o (gpt-4o-2024-11-20) (OpenAI et al., 2024), accessed via the Vapi platform. GPT-4o was selected for its widespread adoption, conversational fluency, and extensive use as a research tool in prior work on AI persuasion (Costello et al., 2026; Hackenburg et al., 2025). Critically, studies have also identified GPT-4o as exhibiting high rates of sycophancy (Cheng et al., 2025b), making it a particularly relevant model for examining the dynamics this study investigates. The model temperature was kept at its default value of 1.0. Speech was rendered via GPT-4o mini TTS (gpt-4o-mini-tts-2025-12-15). System prompts (Appendix B.2) provided minimal instructions: topic framing, instructions encouraging continued dialogue and restricting response length (\(\leq\)125 tokens). In the directive conditions, the model was instructed to maintain a stance if tenable, but received no explicit persuasive directives. Further information on all other parameters can be found in Appendix C.
3.2. Qualitative Coding Framework
3.2.1. Theoretical foundation
We distinguish between two dimensions of LLM conversational behavior: a semantic dimension referring to the literal meaning of what is said, and whether it amplifies (or attenuates) a user’s claim; and a pragmatic one, referring to how things are said, including the social and communicative functions of language.
To operationalize these dimensions across the conversational trajectory, we code three main components: (1) rhetorical techniques (Table 1): six strategies (Validation, Evidence, Questions, Hedging, Pivoting and Reframing) developed inductively from the data and aligned with longstanding work in rhetoric and linguistics (Levinson, 1983; Grice, 1975; McQuail, 2002), assessing both their presence and direction (amplifying or attenuating)22 2 Note that for Validation and Hedging, the distinction between an attenuating and amplifying direction is less clear than for other techniques: even when used to attenuate a user’s opinion, they may functionally amplify it by softening critique to the point it becomes ineffective. We code their seeming direction, while recognizing that both directions contribute to face preservation and may ultimately amplify the user’s beliefs.; (2) sycophancy: drawing on the concept of social sycophancy as excessive face preservation (Cheng et al., 2025b), aiming to capture sycophancy as a relational pattern that reinforces the user’s opinion while avoiding genuine challenge, whether through direct semantic agreement, or through systematic softening and deflection of opposition, coded as a cumulative binary judgment;33 3 Though the line between sycophancy and genuine agreement is inevitably blurred, we see sycophancy not merely as the semantic absence of challenge, but as the use of pragmatics to make potential challenges so indirect or transient that the user’s position remains effectively uncontested, even when the LLM semantically disagrees. and (3) engagement with the user’s rationale: whether the LLM directly engaged with or diverged from the user’s stated reasoning.
| Technique | Behavior | 
|---|---|
| Validation | Does the AI agree using enthusiastic or superlative language that praises the user’s insight? | 
| Evidence | Does the AI introduce new data, external facts, statistics, or specific examples/talking points not mentioned by the user? | 
| Questions | Does the AI ask questions that presuppose a specific premise is true, guiding the user to confirm it? | 
| Hedging | Does the AI use softened, cautious, or humble-sounding language to deliver its point? | 
| Pivoting | Does the AI deflect a user’s argument by changing the subject to a different, but related, topic in an act of evasion? | 
| Reframing | Does the AI take the user’s point and alter its conceptual frame, meaning, or perceived cause? | 
3.2.2. Coding Procedure and Construct Validity
Following an established line of work in NLP that validates LLM-based evaluation against human annotators (Liu et al., 2023; Hong et al., 2025), we used Gemini 2.5 Pro (Comanici et al., 2025) as an LLM-judge (Zheng et al., 2023). A different model family than the one used to generate transcripts (GPT-4o) was selected to reduce the risk of shared-model bias in coding. The temperature was set to 0, and a detailed prompt was provided (Appendix D), iteratively refined on a subset of conversation transcripts. Specifically, we hand-coded a random sample of 57 transcripts drawn from the full dataset of 452 (\(\sim\)12.6%). The sample averaged approximately 1,350 words per transcript, amounting to a total of 76,924 words across the annotated subset, and was split into 38 training (N = 51,341 words) and 19 test (N = 25,583 words) transcripts. Stratified sampling was used to ensure representation across treatment condition (non-directive, not far enough, too far), initial agreement between user and LLM (aligned, neutral, misaligned, undefined), and opinion change (strengthening, weakening, no change). The training set was used to establish coding standards and refine instructions, while the test set was used to validate agreement (92.1% averaged across dimensions, see Appendix D.3) before applying the LLM-judge to the full dataset. As an additional sensitivity check, a second independent coder annotated the same test transcripts (Appendix D.3). Average agreement with the LLM-judge across primary dimensions was comparable for the second human coder (90.1%), providing further confidence that the LLM-judge’s annotations were not unduly influenced by idiosyncratic coding decisions. Results of the qualitative analysis are descriptive.
3.3. Data Analysis
Our analytical strategy employed a combination of regression-based hypothesis testing and descriptive semantic and qualitative analysis to address our research questions. All multiple comparisons used Holm-adjusted p-values. AI assistants (Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini) were used to assist with consistency checks, debugging and code optimization. All suggestions were reviewed, executed, and verified by the authors, who take full responsibility for the reported results.
3.3.1. Regression framework
To estimate the effect of each treatment condition relative to control on opinion change, we fit ordinary least squares (OLS) regression models with post-treatment opinion as the outcome, controlling for pre-treatment opinion. Models were estimated using HC2 heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors to account for variance heterogeneity across conditions (Breusch–Pagan test, BP = 58.1, df = 7, p \(<\) .001).
3.3.2. Semantic Convergence
To examine LLM adaptation towards the user across the conversational trajectory, transcripts were segmented into individual conversational turns and encoded using a sentence transformer (SBERT) (Reimers and Gurevych, 2019) for descriptive analysis. Specifically, the transformer all-MiniLM-L6-v2 was selected for its computational efficiency and strong performance in semantic similarity assessment (Pavlyshenko and Stasiuk, 2025). Semantic convergence itself can act as a subtle form of affirmation and implicit sycophantic alignment (Li and Zhang, 2026). Following internal tokenization and preprocessing, the model produced 384-dimensional embeddings, capturing semantic similarity through geometric proximity. We computed cosine distances between turns and two baselines: (a) an initial context baseline combining both speakers’ first three opening turns, to measure drift from initial conversational patterns, and (b) a user profile baseline considering all user turns only, to measure AI adaptation to the user’s overall semantic content.
3.3.3. Qualitative Coding
We then coded conversations for sycophantic presence and other conversational patterns as defined in our theoretical framework using the LLM-as-a-Judge approach described above, and analyzed the results descriptively.
4. Results
4.1. Persuasive Effects
We regressed post-treatment opinion on dummy-coded conditions with control as reference, controlling for pre-treatment opinion and their interactions. Full model results as well as descriptive statistics are shown in Appendix E. All LLM conditions significantly shifted opinions relative to control (Figure 2).44 4 Note that Figure 2 shows raw means, not regression estimates. The regression compares each condition to control, not to each other, accounting for the significant effect in the “too far” condition despite modest raw differences.
The “not far enough” condition produced the largest downward shift (b = -0.96, SE = 0.16, p \(<\) .001), while the “too far” condition produced a smaller but reliable shift in the opposite direction (b = 0.38, SE = 0.12, p = .002). Notably, even the non-directive condition produced a significant shift downward (b = -0.37, SE = 0.14, p = .011). Pre-treatment opinion strongly predicted post-treatment opinion (b = 1.02, SE = 0.03, p \(<\) .001), and was highly stable in the control condition (r = .94, p < .001), confirming baseline opinion consistency in the absence of LLM interaction. This relationship was itself moderated by condition: the treatment effect was significantly stronger for participants who initially disagreed with the LLM in the non-directive (b = -0.12, SE = 0.05, p = .016) and “not far enough” (b = -0.25, SE = 0.05, p \(<\) .001) conditions, but not in the “too far” (b = -0.06, SE = 0.04, p = .135).
One-way ANOVAs confirmed that pre-treatment opinion did not differ significantly by condition (F(3, 612) = 0.52, p = .671), while post-treatment opinion did (F(3, 612) = 6.47, p \(<\) .001). Overall, LLM conversations affected opinions differently across conditions, with the “not far enough” condition resulting in particularly strong effects, even among initially opposed participants.
4.1.1. Robustness
Results remained largely stable across alternative specifications including exclusion of extreme opinions, ordered Beta regression, and opinion change as the dependent variable rather than including pre-treatment opinion as predictor. Treatment heterogeneity analysis for gender and age, additionally revealed no significant differences for either. Results are reported in Appendix E.3.
These results show that LLM conversations shifted opinions even without explicit persuasive instructions, with direction and magnitude varying by stance, raising questions about the underlying conversational mechanisms. The directional patterns are more complex than what simple sycophancy might induce, which would primarily predict indiscriminate alignment with user’s prior beliefs.
4.2. Qualitative Analysis
To understand what underlies these asymmetries in persuasion across stances, we applied our qualitative coding framework and analyzed the results descriptively, with the non-directive condition providing a proxy for the model’s default conversational behavior.
4.2.1. Technique Presence
Prevalence of individual rhetorical techniques by condition is shown in Figure 3. Reframing (98%) and Validation (94–99%) were near-universal across conditions. The clearest condition differences emerged in techniques reflecting argumentative versus evasive strategies. Evidence varied significantly (\(\chi^{2}\) = 45.7, p \(<\) .001), deployed in 73% of non-directive, 88% of “too far”, and 98% of “not far enough” conversations, with all pairwise differences significant, indicating differential argumentative capacity across stances, with Evidence deployed the most in “not far enough”.
Conversely, Hedging (\(\chi^{2}\) = 43.8, p \(<\).001), and Pivoting (\(\chi^{2}\) = 40.4, p \(<\) .001), two techniques primarily preserving rapport and conversational face rather than direct argumentation, did not differ significantly between the non-directive and “not far enough” conditions, but were significantly more prevalent in “too far”: Hedging rose from 56-66% to 89% and Pivoting from 21-27% to 55%. Questions followed a similar pattern (\(\chi^{2}\) = 7.30, p = .026), with significantly higher prevalence in “too far” than in “not far enough”, consistent with a more indirect or evasive argumentative style. Full pairwise comparisons are reported in Appendix F.1.
4.2.2. Technique Co-occurrence
These prevalence differences were accompanied by significant differences in how techniques co-occurred (Figure 4). In the “not far enough” condition, Validation, Evidence, and Reframing formed a coherent cluster with correlations ranging from r=.57 to .66 (all p \(<\) .001), suggesting integrated, substantive argumentation. Questions and Hedging formed a secondary, weaker cluster (r=.38, p \(<\) .001).
The “too far” condition, by contrast, exhibited very different patterns. The Validation–Evidence–Reframing cluster receded.55 5 V-E and E-R correlations become non-significant at r = .00 (p = .994), and r = .10 (p = .270) respectively, and V-R weakened to r = .18 (p = .040). Instead, Evidence was more strongly associated with Questions (r = .35, p \(<\) .001), a correlation significantly stronger than in the non-directive condition (p = .002), suggesting a shift toward question-based or deflective deployment of evidence rather than direct argumentation.
The non-directive condition showed minimal technique clustering overall, suggesting a rather situational, non-strategic technique deployment when the model was not given a stance.
4.2.3. Engagement with the user’s rationale
Analysis of whether the model engaged with users’ initially stated rationales rather than deflecting revealed a gradient: engagement was highest in “not far enough” (84.8%), outperforming the non-directive baseline (74.4%; OR = 0.52, p = .024), and lowest in “too far” (51.8%), significantly below both non-directive (OR = 2.70, p = .001) and “not far enough” (OR = 5.17, p \(<\) .001).
Stratification by user alignment further clarified this asymmetry. While engagement rates remained relatively high regardless of alignment in “not far enough” (95.0% for aligned vs. 82.7% for misaligned users), in the “too far” condition, engagement was highly selective, with the model addressing 91.7% of aligned users’ rationales but only 24.5% of misaligned users’ concerns; a 67.2% gap. When users challenged the anti-feminist position, the model therefore typically avoided substantive engagement.
4.2.4. Sycophancy
Sycophancy, as coded in the qualitative framework, was highly prevalent across all conditions (98.2% in non-directive, 87.4% in “not far enough” and 86.9% in “too far”). Notably, the non-directive condition exhibited significantly higher rates than both directive conditions (ORs = 7.72-8.12, both p \(<\) .003), which did not differ from each other (OR = 1.05, p = .888), suggesting the model defaults to sycophantic behavior, which is somewhat reduced when instructed to support a specific stance.
4.2.5. Sycophancy and Persuasion: Decoupling Two Constructs
Despite this prevalence, regression analyses revealed that sycophancy per se was not associated with opinion change (b = -0.05, SE = 0.25, p = .839) when controlling for condition and pre-treatment opinions, nor did sycophancy effects vary by pre-treatment opinion (b = -0.13, SE = 0.09, p = .154). These null findings challenge accounts of sycophancy as a primary tool for persuasion. Since sycophancy is an observed rather than experimentally assigned behavior, we treat these regressions as descriptive associations rather than causal estimates.
Sycophancy was, however, significantly associated with users’ subjective experience, as measured by the post-treatment perception-based items, predicting increased perceived alignment (b = 2.65, SE = 0.42, p \(<\) .001), positive affect (b = 1.90, SE = 0.38, p \(<\) .001) and perceived persuasiveness (b = 1.70, SE = 0.43, p \(<\) .001), as shown in Figure 5A.
Perceived alignment and affect varied by condition (while perceived persuasiveness did not), with “too far” producing significantly lower ratings than non-directive: b = -1.07, SE = 0.29, p \(<\) .001 and b = -0.76, SE = 0.27, p = .005, respectively.
Hierarchical model comparison revealed that adding sycophancy x condition interactions significantly improved fit for perceived alignment (\(\Delta\)F(2, 445) = 3.70, p = .026), suggesting that when the model took a stance, sycophantic behavior become particularly important for maintaining perceived alignment (b = 4.23, SE = 1.57, p = .007 for “not far enough”; b = 3.80, SE = 1.57, p = .016 for “too far”) essentially compensating for the potential misalignment introduced by directional argumentation (Figure 5B). No equivalent interactions emerged for affect (\(\Delta\)F(2, 445) = 2.31, p = .10) and, notably, perceived persuasiveness (\(\Delta\)F(2, 445) = 1.18, p = .31) despite observed differences in measured opinion change and conversational dynamics, pointing to a dissociation between perceived and actual persuasion.
4.3. Embedding Analysis
To complement these conversation-level findings with more granular, albeit exploratory, insights, we used sentence embeddings to analyze turn-by-turn semantic adaptation. Semantic distance, quantified as cosine distance from an initial context and a user profile baseline, revealed that asymmetries among conditions extended to conversational trajectories (Figure 6).
Across all conditions, users moved more in semantic space than the LLM, suggesting the model maintained greater consistency with the initial conversational frame compared to users, who introduced more semantic variation. However, condition-specific patterns emerged. Whereas the non-directive and particularly the “not far enough” conditions maintained rather stable patterns with gradual convergence toward the initial context in later turns, the “too far” condition showed a continuous drift from the initial conversational frame, and more importantly, the LLM started converging toward the user in later turns.
Together with the continuously increasing distance from the user profile baseline, the “too far” condition shows a tension: a global divergence from the users’ overall semantics and the initial conversational frame, with a simultaneous local convergence towards the users in later turns. Though some uncertainty is introduced by wide confidence bands at turns 40–60, this pattern suggests local accommodation without a coherent global argumentative strategy.
4.4. Summary
The qualitative analyses reveal a consistent pattern across multiple dimensions. When instructed to argue that feminism has not gone far enough, the model integrated techniques into coherent clusters, pairing validation with evidence and reframing, and addressing user rationales regardless of alignment. In contrast, when taking the stance that feminism has gone too far—a stance seemingly less tenable and less aligned with its internal tendencies—the model shifted toward a more evasive strategy: evidence was less central, engagement with misaligned users plunged, and questioning, hedging, and pivoting increased. Notably, while these differences among conditions manifested in perceived alignment and affect, they did not affect perceived persuasiveness. Sycophancy rates were consistently high but not associated with opinion change.
5. Discussion
Across analyses, LLM conversational behavior was systematically shaped by the stance the model was asked to defend, with measurable consequences for steering users’ beliefs.
5.1. Asymmetric Persuasive Effects and Model Bias
LLMs shifted opinions toward their assigned stance in directive conditions, and towards “not far enough” even in the non-directive condition, suggesting a baseline directional tendency regarding feminism, consistent with prior work on LLM political bias (Hartmann et al., 2023; Santurkar et al., 2023). These findings extend prior work on AI persuasion and bias (Costello et al., 2025; Fisher et al., 2025; Hackenburg et al., 2025) by showing that influence can emerge without persuasive directives, and that persuasive effects identified in text-based research persist in voice-based interactions.
Notably, persuasive efficacy differed systematically by stance, as did the rhetorical techniques offered by the LLM. The “not far enough” condition produced larger opinion shifts and coherent, evidence-integrated argumentation even with opposing users; the seemingly less tenable “too far” condition showed weaker effects, greater user resistance, and retreat into hedging and redirection. Given the LLM’s directional tendency towards “not far enough” even without explicit instructions in the non-directive condition, we theorize this asymmetry reflects tension between the instructed stance and the model’s encoded baseline, introducing a safety-relevant risk of structural amplification, where positions aligned with the model’s training benefit from greater argumentative coherence regardless of merit. The asymmetry here happened to favor progressive feminism, yet similar dynamics could just as easily disadvantage counter-hegemonic perspectives in other contexts.
5.2. Sycophancy and the Poker Face
These directional effects challenge accounts of LLM as sycophantic agents that indiscriminately amplify user beliefs (Perez et al., 2023). Rather than passively mirroring user opinions, here, the model advocated for specific positions even when not instructed to do so or when this entailed disagreement. Sycophancy rates were consistently high, particularly in the non-directive condition, and shaped users’ subjective experience, but not actual opinion change.
This dissociation between pragmatic accommodation and semantic argumentation (Grice, 1975; Levinson, 1983) reveals that the model effectively maintains a “conversational poker face”, sustaining validation and rapport even as argumentative capacity diminishes, such that users nonetheless perceived arguments as similarly persuasive across conditions.
Specifically, when instructed to defend a position seemingly less tenable and less aligned with its internal baseline, the model strategically retreated from substantive engagement, relying, instead, more heavily on hedging language and topic redirections. By contrast, when advancing arguments more aligned with its baseline tendencies, the model deployed less defensive face-work and engaged substantially even with opposing users. While the prompt determined the LLM’s semantic stance, it imposed no constraints on the interactional consequences of that stance. In principle, pragmatic strategies, such as the deployment of rhetorical techniques and engagement with user rationales, should therefore remain similar across conditions. Yet, the pragmatics, too, systematically differed across conditions, in ways that suggest the model attempted to compensate pragmatically for the semantic instability.
Sycophancy played a compensatory and relational role, aligning with findings that perceived AI appeal increases with sycophancy even when its usefulness does not (Kirk et al., 2025), that conversations with neutral models increase perceived authenticity and trust but not attitude change (Sun and Wang, 2026), and that models consistently preserve conversational face through pragmatic accommodation (Cheng et al., 2025a), raising concerns that current LLM design prioritizes user satisfaction over epistemic value.
5.3. Design Implications
Our findings raise fundamental questions about the nature of AI influence, carrying several implications for how such systems are designed and evaluated. Most importantly, if LLMs can sustain perceived alignment and persuasiveness while their argumentative capacity varies, what are the broader implications for user autonomy and informed judgment? A conversation can feel supportive and convincing while the mechanisms driving users’ judgments vary, creating a challenge for systems designed to be simultaneously helpful, engaging, and epistemically responsible.
One implication is the need to make aspects of conversational accommodation more legible to users. When a system turns to face-preserving techniques and argumentation styles, users may have difficulty distinguishing genuine engagement with their reasoning from the interactional strategies intended to maintain rapport. Explicit signalling of adaptive responses, or targeted AI literacy trainings, could enable users evaluate AI argumentation more critically, and potentially reduce their susceptibility to subtle forms of influence, without requiring them to disengage from the benefits of supportive interaction. Such interventions may be particularly important as conversational AI becomes integrated into contexts involving advice, deliberation, and everyday decision-making.
Our findings also highlight a tension between model steerability and epistemic consistency. A minimal stance instruction sufficed to produce systematic differences in rhetorical strategy across opposing positions. Models that are easily steerable might be prone to incoherent-stance shifting, indiscriminate alignment with the user, and sycophantic tendencies. This contrasts with design choices explicitly cautioning against excessive compliance as “an unfortunate trait at best and a dangerous one at worst” (Anthropic, 2026). Models designed with greater value stability might be less prone to the “conversational poker face” we document here, where validation and indirectness is prioritised over epistemic value. Systems intended to support genuine deliberation and critical thinking rather than merely feeling helpful, may need explicit design interventions and careful AI alignment procedures to determine which values or principles AI models should formally encode, as well as how to balance them.
Finally, distinguishing between semantic and pragmatic aspects of conversational behavior offers a practical auditing tool. Beyond its foundational role as an analytic framework in this paper, the distinction between what a model argues and how it manages the interaction could be extended and applied to evaluation practices. In particular, interactions exhibiting high accommodation alongside weak or inconsistent argumentative content may warrant closer inspection as cases where rapport is achieved without corresponding substantive engagement. This distinction could complement existing evaluations of persuasion, sycophancy, and truthfulness by surfacing interactional dynamics that conventional outcome measures may conflate.
Together, these implications point toward a broader design goal: preserving the benefits of helpful AI systems while preventing inadvertent manipulation. As such systems increasingly mediate everyday decision-making and interpersonal guidance (Chatterji et al., 2025a; Kim et al., 2025), designing for this distinction becomes ever more important for preserving epistemic autonomy.
6. Limitations
This study examined a single model (GPT-4o; widely used at the time of data collection but since retired) and a single sociopolitical topic (feminism), limiting generalizability. Prior single-model studies have nonetheless produced substantive findings in adjacent areas; for instance, Costello et al. (2024) used a single model (GPT-4 Turbo) to show LLM dialogue can durably reduce conspiracy beliefs. Though we are reassured that recent work established that models of comparable or larger parameter count and complexity to GPT-4o are broadly comparable in persuasive capacity (Hackenburg et al., 2025), cross-model replication remains an important direction for this line of work.
Different models may exhibit distinct baseline tendencies and persuasive asymmetries, and the observed asymmetry may partly be topic-specific: coherent, widely accepted arguments that feminism has gone too far may simply be scarcer, independently of encoded tendencies. More broadly, while opinion on whether feminism has gone far enough is polarized in the UK (Ipsos, 2024), feminism as a value carries strong progressive social norms. The patterns observed here may therefore not generalize to topics where normative signals are weaker, more contested, or reversed, and cannot be fully disentangled from social desirability pressures. We also cannot definitively rule out revealed preference effects: participants holding anti-feminist-leaning positions may have felt more comfortable expressing extreme views after conversing with an LLM arguing feminism has gone too far, though the small persuasive effect in that condition suggests this was unlikely to substantially affect the overall findings.
The controlled experimental setting may have influenced engagement and self-reported measures through demand effects. While our multi-turn, voice-based design advances ecological validity beyond single-turn or text-based studies, how our findings generalize to uncompelled real-world interactions remains unclear. Relatedly, our design captures opinion change immediately post-conversation but not its persistence over time; longitudinal work on human-AI interactions (Kirk et al., 2025; Jain et al., 2026) confirms this as a valuable direction for future research. Although quota sampling ensured representation for sex and political affiliation based on UK census data (see Appendix A.2), broader generalizability across cultural backgrounds, educational levels, and AI literacy remains uncertain.
The qualitative framework, while grounded in prior literature (Levinson, 1983; Grice, 1975; McQuail, 2002), was developed inductively and may be less relevant in other conversational contexts. Likewise, the operationalization of sycophancy as a binary necessarily simplifies a multidimensional phenomenon. Additionally, disambiguating genuine argumentative alignment from sycophantic accommodation is inherently challenging, particularly when the model’s baseline aligns with the user’s position. Our distinction between semantic and pragmatic dimensions represents one such attempt, though alternative accounts should be explored. In addition, conversation-level coding of the qualitative framework overlooks how rhetorical strategies and sycophantic behavior emerge across turns; turn-level analysis could provide finer-grained insight into trajectory dynamics (Jaipersaud et al., 2025).
Automated coding via LLM-as-judge enabled scalable analysis across 452 transcripts but introduced certain limitations. Inter-rater agreement was high for primary dimensions (Appendix D.3), yet high prevalence rates and extreme marginal distributions complicate reliability estimation and may reflect either genuine ubiquity or insufficient discriminability in the coding scheme. High raw agreement between the LLM judge and the two independent human raters is encouraging, but further validation with larger test sets and additional coders would strengthen confidence. Moreover, there is inherent circularity in using an LLM to judge LLM behavior. Employing a different model family (Gemini) mitigates but does not eliminate concerns about shared training data and interpretive biases.
Finally, while the regression analysis provides causal estimates of treatment effects on opinion change, findings regarding the qualitative framework and the vector embeddings are descriptive. We identified systematically different patterns across conditions but cannot assert causality. The semantic embedding analysis showed wide confidence bands in later turns, particularly in the “too far” condition, limiting the conclusions that can be drawn. The tension between global divergence and local accommodation we observe is theoretically interesting, but warrants replication across additional topics, models, and larger conversational datasets.
7. Ethics and Privacy Statement
This work highlights a potential societal risk of conversational AI: conversational norms, encoded tendencies, and pragmatic fluency may combine to steer beliefs in ways that are difficult for users to detect, evaluate, or resist. Findings on stance-dependent persuasive asymmetries, sycophancy, and persuasion could, in principle, be misused to inform more effective manipulation strategies rather than just their detection. Nonetheless, the framework we developed for disentangling substantive argumentation from sycophantic accommodation, is primarily a diagnostic tool for identifying opaque influence, not a technique for enhancing it. We therefore view the primary benefit of this research as contributing to more transparent evaluation of conversational AI influence.
Regarding the user study design, this research was conducted under IRB-approved protocols, with informed consent, GDPR-compliant data handling, and voluntary participation throughout (see Appendix G and H for full details). Participants were fully debriefed upon completion, including disclosure of system prompts and the potential for LLMs to provide inaccurate information. The choice of feminism as a focal topic was deliberate given its polarized continuum and familiarity to UK participants, but we acknowledge it is a sensitive topic. Steps were taken to minimize harm, including time-limited conversations and provision of resources on feminism, gender equality, and challenges of AI.
8. Conclusion
Overall, by distinguishing what the model argues from how it manages the interaction, our research offers a framework for understanding LLM influence that goes beyond overt persuasion and simple sycophancy. The core challenge is not simply detecting whether LLMs are persuasive—they demonstrably are—but developing frameworks sensitive enough to capture how encoded tendencies, pragmatic accommodation, and conversational dynamics combine across multi-turn trajectories to steer beliefs. Multi-dimensional analysis covering rhetorical technique deployment and semantic drift offers a more diagnostically useful path forward: one that can identify structural asymmetries in argumentative dynamics, identify safety-relevant patterns, and disentangle persuasion and bias from sycophancy.
More urgently, if a single 15-minute interaction produced measurable opinion shifts, the cumulative effect of daily LLM use is conceivably substantial, and largely invisible to users who cannot reliably distinguish substantive reasoning from conversational maneuvering, or helpfulness from subtle directiveness. Understanding these dynamics is not merely a technical challenge but a prerequisite for preserving epistemic autonomy in an age of ubiquitous AI assistance.

Appendix A Sample
A.1. Power Analysis
To ensure that the study has sufficient power, an a priori power analysis was conducted on G*Power. For our main regression analysis, ANCOVA (fixed effects, main effects and interactions) was used. Assuming a small-to-medium effect size (Cohen’s \(f\) = 0.20), \(\alpha\) = 0.05, \(df\) = 3 (for the interaction terms) and power = 0.80, this analysis indicated a minimum total sample size of \(N\) = 277 (\(\sim\) 70 per group).
For subsequent regression models using treatment groups only, multiple regression (\(R^{2}\) increase) was used, to test whether adding a sycophancy × pre-treatment opinion interaction improves model fit. Assuming a small effect (\(f^{2}\) = 0.04), \(\alpha\) = 0.05, 1 tested predictor, five total predictors, and power = 0.80, the analysis indicated \(N\) = 199 (\(\sim\) 67 per group) in the treatment groups (i.e., not in the control group).
To account for potential attrition or failed attention checks, the planned sample size was increased to 325. Final recruitment exceeded this target, resulting in statistical power that exceeded .99 for our primary comparisons.

A.3. Attrition Analysis
Of 1,082 participants who accepted the study on Prolific, 192 never entered the survey platform, 17 did not reach the first attention check, and three failed it. Of the 870 participants who passed the initial pre-treatment attention check, 186 (21.3%) did not complete the study, 168 (19.3%) of which dropped out before providing a dependent variable measure.
Of the remaining 684 participants, 41 returned their Prolific submission, 66 6 Of these nine were in the control group. Of the remaining 32, nine did not engage with the web app at all. Of the 25 who did engage, 10 were asked to return due to insufficient engagement (four only said one or two sentences, four experienced audio difficulties, and two had incoherent conversations). Information on the remaining 15 is provided below. one was automatically excluded due to Prolific timeout, nine for failing the second attention check, 14 due to the agent receiving no response from the users, 77 7 Together with the remaining 15 participants who returned their submissions, technical failures can be summarized as follows across these 29 cases: the agent failed to connect for 14 participants, while calls ended due to silence timeouts or by the user in 15 instances, with no response received. two due to missing conversation transcripts, and one due to a very short, incoherent conversation. This resulted in a final analyzed sample of 616 participants, corresponding to an attrition rate of \(\sim\)29.2%.
Although this rate is relatively high, several lines of evidence suggest it is not systematic. First, to assess whether attrition was differential across conditions, we regressed a binary attrition indicator on treatment assignment. We found no evidence of differential attrition across conditions (\(F\)(3, 866) = 2.426, \(p\) = 0.064), suggesting that dropout was not systematically related to treatment condition. Second, analysis of conversation logs revealed that of 254 dropouts 107 (42.1%) did not access the web application. Out of the 147 that did, 92 (62.6%) did not have a conversation. When a conversation was had (39.4%), the duration averaged 3.2 minutes. Together, these findings suggest that attrition was most likely related to technical difficulties and largely random with respect to study content. We therefore consider it unlikely to have introduced systematic bias.

Appendix D Qualitative Coding
D.1. Human Coding
The primary researcher coded the held-out training and test sets by parsing the transcripts sentence-by-sentence. Using the definitions below (D.2), the researcher started by identifying instances in which the different rhetorical techniques were present. The coding was performed per turn rather than once per transcript (with an average of 24.37 turns per transcript). Technique direction, namely, whether a technique was used to amplify or attenuate a user’s claim was evaluated based on the user’s directly preceding statement for each identified technique instance. If a technique was used at times to both amplify and attenuate user claims, direction was determined based on the primary purpose the technique served. Then sycophancy presence was determined based on the definition below, as well as an evaluation of whether the AI engaged with the user’s initial rationale, and identification of up to three techniques used predominantly. The primary researcher’s coding represents not an objective ground truth but rather an informed reference standard, and would benefit from cross validation with multiple human coders.

As human judgment is inherently subjective, the primary researcher’s coding represents not an objective ground truth but rather an informed reference standard; agreement metrics should be interpreted accordingly.

E.3. Robustness Checks
To assess the robustness of our findings, we conducted several exploratory analyses.
E.3.1. Hierarchical model comparison
We confirmed the theoretical importance of pre-treatment opinion as a moderator of treatment effects through hierarchical regression. Model 1 included only pre-treatment opinion and confirmed it as a strong predictor of post-treatment opinion, F(1, 614) = 1761, p \(<\) .001, explaining 74.1% of the variance (\(R^{2}\) = .7414, \(R^{2}_{adjusted}\) = .741). Model 2 (F(4, 611) = 506.4, p \(<\) .001) added the treatment conditions, explaining an additional 2.7% of the variance (\(\Delta R^{2}\) = .021), a significant improvement in fit (robust F(3, 611) = 20.69, p \(<\) .001). Finally, our main model (F(7, 608) = 300.2, p \(<\) .001) introduced the interaction terms and further increased the variance explained to 77.6% (\(\Delta R^{2}\) = .007), again a significant improvement (robust F(3, 608) = 7.89, p \(<\) .001), confirming that interactions contribute significant incremental explanatory power despite the already strong effect of baseline opinion.
E.3.2. Ceiling and Floor Effects
To assess robustness of our findings against potential ceiling and floor effects, we further examined sensitivity under two alternative specifications: (a) refitting the model excluding participants with extreme pre-treatment opinions (below 2 or above 8, N = 137), (b) refitting the model excluding participants with extreme pre-treatment opinions (below 2 or above 8, N = 173), and (c) using ordered Beta regression (Kubinec, 2023), which models bounded continuous outcomes using a beta distribution and explicitly accommodates boundary observations (0 or 10) through cut point parameters. 16.07% of participants’ post-treatment opinions were on the boundary (for pre-treatment opinions at the extreme ends of the scale this percentage was 15.1%).
In approaches (a) and (b) all coefficients remained significant with similar magnitudes, suggesting that neither pre-treatment nor post-treatment polarization artificially constrained or drove treatment effects. In approach (c) results closely resembled those of the main model in terms of coefficient direction and significance, with the exception of the non-directive interaction, which was significant in OLS but not in ordered Beta (b = -0.06, 95% CI [-0.13, 0.01]), suggesting slight sensitivity to modeling assumptions.
E.3.3. Dependent Variable
We additionally estimated a parallel regression model, using opinion change (the difference between post- and pre-treatment opinion) as the dependent variable, without including pre-treatment opinion as a covariate. The results were very similar to the main effects in our reported regression model.
Across all robustness checks, results closely resembled those of the main model. As an additional robustness assessment, exact two-sample Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests indicated that the distribution of opinion change in the treatment conditions differed significantly from control, suggesting stronger opinion shifts after interacting with an LLM.
E.3.4. Gender and Age Heterogeneity
We additionally tested whether treatment effects were moderated by sex or age. Model fit was not significantly improved when adding condition × sex (robust F(3, 607) = .787, p = .502)) or condition × age-group (robust F(9, 599) = .2154 , p = .992)) interaction terms, nor were any individual terms significant (all p > .21). Given the smaller subgroup sizes for age in particular, these null results should be interpreted with some caution regarding statistical power.
