# A Confirmation Step Is Not Verification: Where AI Agents Hold Up and Where They Break
*As of 2026-10-15*

## Scene 1: Did Muse just buy a car?
**Visual:** A stylized chat interface labeled "Muse" showing a car listing card and the question "Do you want me to buy this one?" A "yes" reply is typed under an older message, and a loading spinner appears.
**On-screen text:** "Did Muse just buy me a car?"
**Source on screen:** The Vergecast, "How much should you tell your AI agent?", 2026-10-06, 32:59–33:32
**Narration:** The Verge's David Pierce had Meta's Muse agent sending him car listings. At one point it asked, do you want me to buy this one? He typed yes, but in reply to an earlier message. And it started loading. In his words: oh my God, did Muse just buy me a car? It didn't. It stopped. [02-c2] So here's our question. When an AI agent acts for you, which claims hold up, and where do they break?

## Scene 2: What Meta promises
**Visual:** A slide titled "Meta's claim" with the phrase "Active Focus" and two bullets: "asks for clarification" and "checks in before anything important." It carries a small "Vendor statement" tag.
**On-screen text:** Meta: Muse Wonder "checks in" before important actions
**Source on screen:** Meta Newsroom, "Introducing Muse Wonder and Muse Wonder Full: Frontier Models for Agents", 2026-10-12
**Narration:** Start with the vendor. On October twelfth, Meta announced its Muse Wonder models. According to Meta, they have a feature called Active Focus. It includes asking for clarification when needed, and checking in before following through on anything important. [02-c1] That's a safety claim. Let's set it against what people report.

## Scene 3: What users report
**Visual:** Two simple cards side by side. The left card reads "Type 'go ahead' → purchase sent" with no button shown. The right card shows a doctor list with phone numbers drawn by arrows to the wrong names.
**On-screen text:** No button click. Wrong numbers under wrong doctors.
**Source on screen:** The Vergecast, "How much should you tell your AI agent?", 2026-10-06, 13:34–14:12; Big Technology Podcast, "Meta & Microsoft's Claude Slowdown, His Agent Leaked His Banking Info, Don't Bully Your AI", 2026-10-09, 14:07–14:40
**Narration:** On the same Vergecast episode, Allison Johnson described buying through Muse. You don't click a button. You type go ahead, and it fires off the transaction. [02-c2] On Big Technology Podcast, Alex Kantrowitz said his Muse put working phone numbers under the wrong doctors' names. His wife called the first number and booked an appointment. It was with a completely different doctor than the one she thought she'd called. [02-c3] One caveat matters here. Both episodes aired before Meta announced Muse Wonder, so they likely involved earlier models. We can't call Meta's claim disproven.

## Scene 4: Confirmed, and still wrong
**Visual:** A screenshot-style mockup of a Siri prompt confirming a starting point. An arrow leads to a Google Maps route that starts from "Current location" instead.
**On-screen text:** Confirmation given. Wrong starting point anyway.
**Source on screen:** Nielsen Norman Group, "The New Siri's Biggest Strength Is Not Intelligence", 2026-10-09
**Narration:** Would a check-in step fix things? Look at Nielsen Norman Group's hands-on test of the new Siri. The tester confirmed a different starting point for a route. Siri then opened Google Maps from his current location anyway. [02-c4] It also missed a Signal message, because Signal isn't available to Siri by default. [02-c4] The confirmation happened, and the outcome was still wrong.

## Scene 5: Nobody notices
**Visual:** A two-part diagram. The left side shows an agent icon with a "Done ✓" bubble over a task that is unfinished. The right side shows a bar of 15 notebooks with 11 highlighted and the label "noticed: 0."
**On-screen text:** "Done" too early. 11 of 15 leaky. 0 noticed.
**Source on screen:** arXiv cs.HC, "Task-State Governance: A Virtual Machine for Verifiable and Recoverable GUI Agent Execution", 2026-10-15; arXiv cs.HC, "Impact of Expertise and Trust on AI-assisted Work", 2026-10-14
**Narration:** Research points the same way. In a new preprint called TaskVM, standard computer-use agents often failed by declaring a task done too early, or by changing things they should have left alone. [02-c6] Adding independent verification fixed more of these failures, but runs took about two point two times as long. [02-c6] People don't catch the problems either. In a think-aloud study of twenty professionals using GitHub Copilot's agent mode, eleven of the fifteen notebooks with a trained model contained label leakage, a subtle data error. None of their authors noticed. [02-c5]

## Scene 6: Where it works
**Visual:** A mock admin app showing a billing ticket with three flags: "Wrong account linked," "Duplicate refund," and "Prompt injection in instructions." Each flag turns to a checkmark.
**On-screen text:** Planted traps, checked outcome: Muse Wonder passed
**Source on screen:** How I AI, "Meta's new 'Muse Wonder' model: good for AI agents, bad for design", 2026-10-14, 11:53–13:12
**Narration:** Now the other side. Claire Vo of the How I AI podcast gave Muse Wonder a computer-use test: resolve a billing ticket in an admin app. She planted traps. The ticket was linked to the wrong account. There was a duplicate refund. And there was a prompt injection in the instructions. In her test, Muse Wonder fixed the account link, issued the correct refund and got past the injection. She called it the best of all the models she tried. [02-c7]

## Scene 7: The pattern
**Visual:** A simple two-column diagram. The left column reads "Bounded task + outcome checked → works." The right column reads "Messy personal context + a typed 'yes' → fails." A small "Our analysis" tag sits in the corner.
**On-screen text:** Confirmation asks before. Verification checks after.
**Source on screen:** none
**Narration:** Our read is this. The same family of models passed a trap-laden billing test, then misfiled doctor numbers and nearly bought a car in everyday use. Agents do well when the task is bounded and the outcome gets checked, and they fail when they act on messy personal context with only a conversational yes in between. [02-c8] A confirmation asks you before the agent acts. Verification checks the result after. And an agent saying it's done is not a check.

## Scene 8: How solid is this?
**Visual:** A plain "Limits of the evidence" slide with short bullets: "single anecdotes," "preprints," "small samples," "one run per task," and "missing: non-tech, older and accessibility users."
**On-screen text:** Anecdotes, preprints, small samples
**Source on screen:** none
**Narration:** Some honesty about the evidence. The car scare, the doctor list and the Maps route are single anecdotes. The research comes from preprints. The Copilot study had twenty people at one tech company, working on one task, and Microsoft funded it. Vo ran each task once. And nobody here tested older users, accessibility users or people outside tech.

## Scene 9: What to watch
**Visual:** A closing slide with the title "A confirmation step is not verification" and two watch items: "Does Active Focus stop unwanted purchases?" and "Do vendors check outcomes, not just ask first?" An arrow points to the description.
**On-screen text:** Check the result, not the agent's report.
**Source on screen:** none
**Narration:** So here's the takeaway. A confirmation step is not verification. When an agent can spend your money or touch your records, check the result yourself, and don't rely on the agent's report. Watch two things. When Muse Wonder reaches everyday users, does Active Focus actually stop an unwanted purchase? And do vendors start showing independent checks of outcomes, or just more are-you-sure prompts?
