# A confirmation step is not verification

*Agents mostly do what vendors say on bounded tasks. The "safely on your behalf" part is where the evidence gets thin.*

*As of 2026-10-15 · Edition evidence window: 2026-09-16 to 2026-10-15*

David Pierce was letting Meta's Muse agent send him car listings. On The Vergecast, he said it eventually asked, "Do you want me to buy this one?" He typed yes, but in reply to an earlier message, and something started loading. "Oh my God, did Muse just buy me a car?" It hadn't, and the process stopped (The Vergecast, 32:59) [02-c2].

That moment is a good test of this month's claims about agents acting for you. When the task is bounded and checkable, vendor descriptions of what agents can do mostly hold up. Claims that agents act *safely* on your behalf hold up less well. The failures that testers report cluster where an agent works from loose personal context. The research suggests that neither the agent nor its user reliably notices when the work is wrong.

## The promise and the shipped product

Meta says its new Muse Wonder models have "Active Focus." That includes "asking for clarification when needed and checking in before following through on anything important" [02-c1].

Earlier in the same Vergecast episode, Allison Johnson described something closer to the opposite in the Muse she used. Once your card is stored, "you don't even have to click a button. You just type the words, go ahead. And it just fires off the transaction for you" (13:34) [02-c2]. She called this "the product working as intended" [02-c2].

On Big Technology Podcast, Alex Kantrowitz said his Muse put working phone numbers under the wrong doctors' names. As a result, his wife booked an appointment with a different doctor from the one she thought she had called (14:07) [02-c3].

This should not be scored as Meta caught out. Both episodes aired before Meta's October 12 announcement, so they most likely describe earlier Muse models, not Muse Wonder. The conflict may simply be a version difference.

## Why a check-in is not a check

Even if Muse Wonder checks in as promised, other evidence suggests that a confirmation step alone may not close the gap.

In Nielsen Norman Group's hands-on test of Siri AI, the tester confirmed a different starting point. Siri then opened Google Maps with a route from his current location in Durham, NC, anyway [02-c4]. In the same test, Siri missed a message in Signal because Signal "is not available to Siri by default" [02-c4]. The confirmation happened, and the result was still wrong.

The research points the same way. In the TaskVM benchmark, standard computer-use agents frequently failed through "premature completion claims and collateral modifications." In plain terms, they said "done" too early or changed things they should have left alone [02-c6]. Adding independent verification fixed more of these failures, but it took about 2.2 times as long [02-c6].

Users don't reliably catch the errors either. In a think-aloud study of 20 professionals using GitHub Copilot's agent mode, 11 of the 15 notebooks that included a trained model contained label leakage, a quiet flaw that makes a model look better than it is. None of those notebooks' authors noticed, and they included four experts as well as seven novices [02-c5].

## When it does work

Claire Vo's test shows the other side. On How I AI, she described a computer-use task in which the agent had to resolve a billing ticket in an admin app. The ticket was full of planted traps: a link to the wrong account, an odd refund amount with a duplicate, and a prompt injection in the instructions (How I AI, 11:53) [02-c7]. She reports that Muse Wonder fixed the account link, issued the correct refund and resisted the injection. "It was the best of all of them," she said (12:25) [02-c7].

Put together, the sources suggest a pattern. The same Muse family passed a trap-laden test with a defined correct outcome, yet in everyday use it misfiled doctors' numbers and nearly bought a car while working from messy personal context [02-c8]. Our reading is that reliability tracks whether someone, or something, independently checks the outcome. A confirmation dialog, or the agent's own report that it has finished, does not do that job [02-c8].

## What this evidence can't settle

The consumer failures are single anecdotes: one car scare, one doctor list, one Maps route. The research is preprints with small or narrow samples. The Copilot study had 20 participants at one tech company working on one task, and Microsoft funded it. TaskVM's user study replayed fixed agent runs, so it measures how people perceive agent work rather than what happens in live use. Vo's result is one practitioner's single run per task. We also set aside a reported Slack leak involving an agent, because it reaches us second-hand through a podcast. None of this testing includes users outside tech, older users or accessibility users.

So treat this as a working hypothesis, not a verdict. Before you let an agent make a purchase or book an appointment, ask what will check the result besides your "yes" and the agent's "done."

Here is what to watch. Hands-on reviews of Muse Wonder will show whether it actually pauses before purchases. More importantly, watch whether any agent shows you the outcome in a form you can check, not just a cheerful confirmation that it worked.
