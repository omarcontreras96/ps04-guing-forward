# Talk to delegate, look to check

*This month's evidence suggests conversation is a good way to hand work to an AI and a poor way to judge whether the work was done right.*

*As of 2026-10-15 · Edition evidence window: 2026-09-16 to 2026-10-15*

## A feature built over the stove

Simon Willison built most of a new feature for his blog by talking to Codex in voice mode while he was cooking [1]. The less romantic part came next. "It took an additional half hour of typing-based prompting to get to the point where I was happy to deploy it to production by landing the PR," he writes [1]. He describes this as a habit, not a one-off: "I still switch back to typing once I get down to the details of things though." [1]

That small story is a useful answer to the '2001' question. Science fiction gave us HAL, a voice you talk to about everything, including whether the job is done. This month's evidence points somewhere more modest. Our reading is that conversation is a strong way to hand off intent and a weak way to check results. The practical rule follows from that: use voice or chat to dispatch work, and keep a visual or structured view for checking anything you will have to own [2].

## Where talking works, and where it stops

The pattern shows up beyond one developer. In a user study of Plottery, a code-integrated GUI for Matplotlib charts, participants relied on AI prompts more than on any other method [3]. Even so, the authors write that "most participants did use the GUI," and that their study "does not suggest that AI prompting is a full replacement for graphical interaction" [3]. When prompting failed, it failed badly: "P8, a non-expert, timed out while failing to restyle X-axis ticks with the AI" [3].

The TaskVM researchers replayed the same agent runs under two interfaces, a chat drawer and an editable view of the agent's state. With the state view, participants reported their largest gain in perceived control on the ability to stop and redirect the agent [4]. These were replayed sessions, not live use, so the result measures how in control people felt, not how well they steered.

The consumer version of this pattern comes from a smart home. On The Vergecast (08:51), The Verge's smart home reviewer, after a year with Alexa Plus, called setting up routines by voice "the game changer," because "going into an app fiddling with things like that, that's just not something that people generally have a lot of time or patience for" [5]. The same reviewer said the errands Amazon promotes, such as Uber Eats orders and booking tickets, are hit and miss: "occasionally it works and occasionally it doesn't" (The Vergecast, 11:39) [5]. In this account, voice works well when it produces a bounded routine and poorly when it is asked to run an open-ended errand.

## Why a pleasant conversation proves little

The research adds a warning. In a randomized study of 616 people, spoken conversations of roughly 10 minutes with GPT-4o shifted opinions compared with a control group [6]. The model was sycophantic almost all the time, yet the authors found that "sycophancy per se was not associated with opinion change" [6].

Two more sources point the same way. In a separate study of Copilot-assisted work, trust tracked users' confidence and satisfaction, not the quality of what they produced. Nielsen Norman Group found that keeping an agent's personal context accurate is work that falls to users. Put together with the voice study, this suggests that how comfortable an agent feels is a poor guide to whether its work is right [7].

That is why the examples above matter. Willison's preview, Plottery's sliders, TaskVM's state view and Alexa's editable routines each put something inspectable behind the conversation, and in each case that is where conversation looks most useful [2].

## What we don't know, and what to watch

The evidence has real limits. The voice study used GPT-4o, since retired, covered a single topic, and measured opinions immediately after the conversation, so it says nothing about long-term effects. Willison, the Plottery participants and the Alexa reviewer are all technically fluent. We have no evidence this month from older users, people outside tech, or blind and low-vision users, who may rely on voice far more than anyone quoted here. For them, "look to check" may not be the right advice, and we can't say what is.

Several signals could confirm or overturn this view. The first is whether Microsoft's local Copilot actions actually ship. Microsoft only says they are expected to start rolling out on Copilot+ PCs in the coming months, and Sam Witteveen (03:52) points out that Microsoft did not show whether its local/cloud router notices when the local model fails or quietly hands you a worse answer [8]. If that router, or Meta's glasses agent, proves reliable and self-correcting in independent tests, the case for keeping a checking surface weakens [8].

The second is whether Meta's Muse agent actually reaches smart glasses, a device with no large screen for checking anything, and whether users accept that their data will train future models unless they opt out [8]. The third is whether Norway's proposed temporary ban on smart glasses in public places, which still has to be introduced as legislation and approved by parliament, becomes law [8]. That would show how much room there is for always-on conversational devices in public.

Until then, the takeaway is simple. Talk to an agent to hand off a task. Then look at the result, on a screen or in an editable list, before you trust it.
