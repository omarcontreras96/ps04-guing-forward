# Talk to Delegate, Look to Check: The '2001' Question in Practice

*As of 2026-10-15*

## Scene 1: Cooking with Codex
**Visual:** A kitchen counter illustration with a laptop open beside a pot. Below it, a text excerpt from the blog post "A new feature for my blog, built using my voice," with no photos of the author.
**On-screen text:** Built by voice. Finished by typing.
**Source on screen:** Simon Willison's Weblog, "A new feature for my blog, built using my voice," 2026-10-09
**Narration:** Blogger Simon Willison built most of a new feature for his blog by talking to Codex in voice mode while he was cooking. [03-c1] Then it took another half hour of typing before he was happy to ship it. [03-c1] That gap is this month's answer to an old science fiction question. Since HAL in 2001: A Space Odyssey, we have imagined talking to our computers. So is conversation the best way to use AI, or just the best way to start?

## Scene 2: HAL was the wrong model
**Visual:** A stylized glowing red circle on black, not a film still. It splits into two labeled arrows: "Hand off" points to a speech bubble, and "Check" points to a screen icon.
**On-screen text:** Talk to delegate. Look to check.
**Source on screen:** none
**Narration:** Our read of this month's evidence is that HAL was the wrong model. Talking is turning out to be a great way to hand off what you want, and a poor way to check what you got. A pleasant exchange feels right whether or not the work is.

## Scene 3: Typing for the details
**Visual:** A pull quote card with Willison's line. It transitions to the Plottery paper's title page and a simple chart whose horizontal axis tick marks are highlighted.
**On-screen text:** "Not a full replacement for graphical interaction"
**Source on screen:** Simon Willison's Weblog, 2026-10-09; arXiv cs.HC, "Plottery: A Code-Integrated GUI for Matplotlib," 2026-10-14
**Narration:** Start with Willison himself. In his words, I still switch back to typing once I get down to the details of things though. [03-c1] Researchers saw a similar pattern in a study of Plottery, a code-integrated graphical tool for making charts. Participants relied on AI prompts more than any other method. [03-c2] But one non-expert ran out of time while failing to get the AI to restyle the tick marks on a chart's horizontal axis. [03-c2] Most participants still used the graphical controls, and the authors say their study does not suggest prompting is a full replacement for graphical interaction. [03-c2]

## Scene 4: Seeing the agent's state
**Visual:** A side-by-side diagram. On the left, a narrow chat drawer. On the right, a panel listing an agent's task steps, each with "edit," "stop" and "redirect" buttons.
**On-screen text:** Biggest control gain: stop and redirect
**Source on screen:** arXiv cs.HC, "Task-State Governance: A Virtual Machine for Verifiable and Recoverable GUI Agent Execution," 2026-10-15
**Narration:** A second study, TaskVM, replayed the same agent runs under two interfaces: a chat drawer, and an editable view of the agent's state. [03-c3] With the state view, participants reported their biggest gain in feeling in control on one thing: being able to stop and redirect the agent. [03-c3] Those were replayed sessions, not live use, so treat this as a design signal, not a verdict.

## Scene 5: Alexa Plus, a year in
**Visual:** A generic smart speaker icon beside a phone mockup of a routine list. A second panel shows food delivery and ticket icons with a yellow warning sign.
**On-screen text:** Voice routines: yes. Errands: sometimes.
**Source on screen:** The Vergecast, "The many pluses and minuses of Alexa Plus," 2026-10-08, 08:51–09:27 and 11:39–12:14
**Narration:** Then there's Alexa Plus. The Verge's smart home reviewer, after a year of testing, calls setting up routines with your voice the game changer, because fiddling in an app is not something people have much patience for. [03-c4] But the agentic errands Amazon has promised, like Uber Eats orders or booking trips and tickets, often hit a roadblock. [03-c4] In the reviewer's words, occasionally it works and occasionally it doesn't. [03-c4]

## Scene 6: Feeling right versus being right
**Visual:** Two simple gauges side by side. One is labeled "How it feels" and reads high. The other is labeled "How good the work is" and shows a question mark. Small captions name the two papers.
**On-screen text:** Comfort is not correctness
**Source on screen:** arXiv cs.HC, "The AI's Poker Face," 2026-10-14; arXiv cs.HC, "Impact of Expertise and Trust on AI-assisted Work," 2026-10-14
**Narration:** Now the warning. In a randomized study, six hundred and sixteen people held spoken conversations of about ten minutes with GPT-4o. [03-c5] Every AI condition shifted opinions compared with a control group. [03-c5] The model was sycophantic almost all the time, yet that sycophancy was not associated with how much opinions changed. [03-c5] Our read is that how comfortable an agent feels is a poor guide to whether its work is right, because in a separate study of Copilot users, trust tracked confidence, not the quality of their work. [03-c6] One caveat: the voice study used a now-retired model, on a single topic, and measured opinions right after the chat.

## Scene 7: Dispatcher, not oracle
**Visual:** A four-tile grid: a code preview window, chart sliders, an agent state panel, and an editable routine list. A speech bubble arrow feeds into all four.
**On-screen text:** Voice to dispatch. A screen to check.
**Source on screen:** none
**Narration:** The practical takeaway is to use voice or chat to dispatch work, and a visual or structured view to check it. [03-c7] In Willison's preview, Plottery's controls, TaskVM's state view and Alexa's editable routines, conversation worked best with something you could inspect sitting behind it. [03-c7] One limit applies. Willison, the Plottery participants and the Alexa reviewer are all technically fluent. We have no evidence from older users, people outside tech, or blind and low-vision users, who may rely on voice far more.

## Scene 8: Three signals to watch
**Visual:** A checklist slide with three rows: a Windows laptop icon labeled "Local and cloud," a glasses icon labeled "Muse," and a map pin over Norway labeled "Proposed ban."
**On-screen text:** Copilot rollout. Muse on glasses. Norway's vote.
**Source on screen:** Windows Blog, "Building Windows for hybrid intelligence," 2026-10-07; Sam Witteveen, "Microsoft Joins the Local AI Push," 2026-10-08, 03:52–04:23; UploadVR, 2026-09-29; Road to VR, 2026-10-07
**Narration:** Three signals could confirm or overturn this view. First, Copilot on Windows. Watch whether its local features roll out, which Microsoft says is expected to begin in the coming months. [03-c8] Also watch whether its handoff between local and cloud models visibly handles its own failures, something Sam Witteveen notes was not shown. [03-c8] Second, watch whether Meta's Muse agent reaches its glasses, where Meta will use the data to train future models unless you opt out. [03-c8] Third, watch whether Norway's parliament passes a proposed temporary ban on smart glasses in public places, which still has to be introduced as legislation. [03-c8] If Microsoft's router or Meta's glasses agent proves reliable and self-correcting in independent tests, our argument weakens.

## YouTube description
Is talking to AI, the way HAL worked in 2001, the best way to use it? This month's evidence suggests conversation is a strong way to hand off work and a weak way to check it. A blogger's voice-built feature, two HCI studies, a year with Alexa Plus and new research on trust all point the same way. Use voice or chat as a dispatcher, and keep a visual or structured view for anything you need to own.
