# The agent became the front door; the hardware is still auditioning

*This month's biggest change sits inside the apps you already use, not in a new gadget.*

*As of 2026-10-15 · Edition evidence window: 2026-09-16 to 2026-10-15*

Ethan Mollick's airline credit was about to expire, and he hadn't noticed. Muse, Meta's personal agent, did. When he asked, it contacted American Airlines to request an extension [01-c2]. Another of his agents messaged him because an email he had sent to his town about a permit had the wrong project number on it [01-c2].

That is one user's account on his own newsletter, not a measurement. It still captures the most consequential shift of the past month. Agents that hold personal context and act on it are moving from side experiments to the default entry point of mainstream apps. The OS agents and new devices announced this month matter mostly as places for those agents to run, and most of them are still promises.

## The agent moved into the main app

The clearest example is Google's. It has merged Spark, its personal agent, into the main Gemini chat, so ordinary chats and Spark tasks now share one history [01-c1]. The agent is no longer a separate destination you have to remember to visit. It is there when you open the app [01-c1]. There are real limits. The redesign is rolling out on Android only, and Spark requires an AI Pro or AI Ultra subscription [01-c1].

Mollick's examples show what this kind of agent is for. It doesn't wait for a prompt; it notices something in your life and raises it. One detail matters. Muse spotted the expiring credit on its own, but it contacted the airline only after Mollick asked [01-c2]. The noticing was proactive. The acting waited for permission.

## The OS agents are mostly on the calendar

Microsoft's Windows news this month follows a familiar pattern. What shipped is the fence. Microsoft Execution Containers (MXC) are now generally available on Windows 11, letting organizations define which files and networks agents can access, with those policies enforced at runtime [01-c3]. The agent the fence is built for has not arrived. Copilot features that use local context and take actions "are expected to begin rolling out on Copilot+ PCs in the coming months," in Microsoft's words [01-c3].

Google's Googlebook has the AI built in, but the headline feature isn't the one that earned its keep. After a week with the laptop, Marques Brownlee found that Magic Pointer, which lets you wiggle the cursor and ask Gemini about whatever you select, is "actually off by default" (MKBHD, 03:25) [01-c4]. "I think my personal favorite is actually the semantic file search," he said. You describe what you remember is in a file, and the Files app finds it [01-c4]. The most useful AI on the machine was the plainest.

## Devices are auditioning as hosts

Apple's HomeView is built around knowing who is standing in front of it. Brownlee reports that it switches to your account, including your messages and calendar, when its single 12-megapixel camera recognizes your face and your iPhone or Watch was recently unlocked nearby (MKBHD, 05:15) [01-c5]. It has no infrared and no voice recognition. So it can give you personal answers only if it sees you, "not if you're around the corner or on the couch" [01-c5]. He also wondered how well it will recognize people in the dark [01-c5].

On The Vergecast (57:22), the hosts summed up the month's hardware this way: "everyone's hardware idea boils down to we need to give you surface on which to let your AI agent do things on your behalf" [01-c7]. Meta's plan for Muse on its glasses fits that description. You make a spoken request, and the agent will "work in the background and check back when the task is finished" [01-c7]. Put together with Copilot's local actions still months out, this suggests that this month's new hardware mostly serves as a host for agents rather than offering new ways to interact on its own [01-c7].

Google's Guided Vision is one device-plus-agent combination aimed at a specific need. Google says this voice-and-camera assistant in Gemini Live for blind and low-vision users is now available on Android 9 and above, and says it "can make mistakes." No independent user reports exist yet [01-c8].

The backlash, meanwhile, has fixed on the camera. Norway plans to propose a temporary ban on smart glasses in public places such as parks, beaches, museums, shopping malls and public events, though it must still pass as legislation through parliament [01-c6]. Dutch eyewear chain Hans Anders halted sales of Meta smart glasses, citing privacy concerns, and Meta opened preorders for camera-free Ray-Ban Meta Audio glasses at $349, shipping from October 13 [01-c6]. None of these responses address the other thing these agents run on: access to your accounts [01-c6].

## What we can't tell yet, and what to watch

Muse dominates this story because it dominated the news. The number of articles reflects news volume, not independent confirmation. No source offers adoption or retention numbers for Muse, Spark or any of the OS assistants. Nobody has independently tested Copilot's local actions, Muse on glasses, or Guided Vision, and Guided Vision is documented only by Google. Non-US perspectives are thin, limited to Norway and the Netherlands. The ranking above is our judgment of consequence, not a measured result.

Signals that would confirm or overturn this reading:

- Whether Spark's merged chat spreads beyond Android and paid tiers.
- Whether Copilot's local actions ship and hold up in independent testing.
- How Norway's parliament votes.
- Whether blind and low-vision users report that Guided Vision is reliable enough to depend on.

If agents stay the front door while the devices keep trading features, this month's ranking will look right.
