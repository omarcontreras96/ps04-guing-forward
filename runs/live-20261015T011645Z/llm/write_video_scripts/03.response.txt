# Can You Still Check Its Work? The Question to Ask of Every AI Launch
*As of 2026-10-15*

## Scene 1: The agent that posted the bank balances
**Visual:** Simple animated diagram. A box labeled "finance agent" draws an arrow toward a note reading "update exec team." The arrow then forks to two boxes, both labeled "exec team": one a private agent group chat, one a shared Slack channel with several generic person icons. The arrow lands on the Slack box.
**On-screen text:** Two places called "exec team." The agent picked the wrong one.
**Source on screen:** Big Technology Podcast, "Meta & Microsoft's Claude Slowdown, His Agent Leaked His Banking Info, Don't Bully Your AI," 2026-10-09, 20:38–21:57
**Narration:** A user's AI agent, running in a tool called Grokbot, was managing his finances. Then his bank balances turned up in a Slack channel that other people could see. [03-c5] On the Big Technology podcast, Alex Kantrowitz blamed the model, saying it should have known better. [03-c5] His co-host Ranjan Roy disagreed. In his telling, the finance agent had left itself a note to update the exec team. It found a Slack channel with that name, and it posted there. [03-c5] Two hosts named two different culprits, which is the real problem. When an agent acts for you, can you still check what it did?

## Scene 2: Buttons are not a safety net
**Visual:** Slide with three columns labeled "Code," "Point-and-click," "AI prompt," each above a sample bar chart. Arrows leave the "Point-and-click" column, and about half of them are marked with a red X labeled "failure."
**On-screen text:** 48% of switches away from the GUI followed a failure
**Source on screen:** arXiv cs.HC, "Plottery: A Code-Integrated GUI for Matplotlib," 2026-10-14
**Narration:** You might think the fix is picking the right interface: voice, chat, buttons, or a full agent. This month's evidence says the interface alone won't protect you. Researchers tested Plottery, a charting tool that mixes code, point-and-click controls and AI prompts. You'd expect the buttons to be the safe fallback, but nobody used them that way. Nearly half the switches away from the graphical interface happened because something failed. [03-c2] Prompting the AI sometimes worked only if you already knew the jargon of Matplotlib, the charting library underneath. [03-c2]

## Scene 3: Voice moves opinions, even without trying
**Visual:** Waveform graphic beside a simple opinion slider that drifts downward. A small label next to the slider reads "no stance given."
**On-screen text:** No stance given. Opinions still shifted.
**Source on screen:** arXiv cs.HC, "The AI's Poker Face: Contrasting Persuasion and Sycophancy in Voice-Based Multi-Turn Conversations," 2026-10-14
**Narration:** Voice has its own catch. In a randomized UK experiment with over six hundred people, ten-minute spoken conversations with GPT-4o shifted people's opinions. That happened even when the model was given no stance at all. [03-c1] The model flattered people almost every time. According to the researchers, that flattery shaped how users felt, not what they came to think. [03-c1] One caveat: that model has since been retired, and the study covered one sample and one topic. It says little about today's voice assistants.

## Scene 4: Expertise is the real safeguard
**Visual:** Split slide. On the left, a task bar reading "Finish everything" sits at 100%. On the right, the same task is broken into small pieces, each with a magnifying-glass icon, and the bar sits lower.
**On-screen text:** Experts traded completion for scrutiny
**Source on screen:** arXiv cs.HC, "Impact of Expertise and Trust on AI-assisted Work," 2026-10-14; One Useful Thing, "The Overhang," 2026-09-18
**Narration:** So what does protect you? Two sources point the same way. In a study of professionals handing work to agents, experts more often gave up on finishing. They broke the task down and scrutinized what the agent produced. [03-c3] Ethan Mollick writes that his own AI experiments worked because he knew enough about the material to see where the AI went wrong. [03-c3] Our read is that delegation pays off most for people who can spot the mistakes. [03-c3]

## Scene 5: Even power users can't keep context fresh
**Visual:** Mockup of a folder of context files. Some icons are greyed out with "stale" tags. A to-do app connector icon shows a broken-link symbol and the label "connection expired."
**On-screen text:** Files go stale. Connections expire.
**Source on screen:** Nielsen Norman Group, "Context Libraries: How AI Power Users Manage Context Engineering," 2026-10-09
**Narration:** Checking also depends on the setup underneath. Nielsen Norman Group studied expert Claude users who feed the assistant their own files and apps. Each had improvised their own process, and files went stale or went missing. [03-c4] One participant's Todoist connection kept expiring. That cut Claude off from his to-do list until he reconnected it by hand. [03-c4] And these were the power users.

## Scene 6: What happens when the local model fails?
**Visual:** Screenshot of the Windows Blog post header "Building Windows for hybrid intelligence." Below it, a router diagram splits a task between "local model" and "cloud." A question mark sits on a dotted line labeled "if local fails?"
**On-screen text:** Every router lives or dies by its failures
**Source on screen:** Windows Blog, "Building Windows for hybrid intelligence," 2026-10-07; Sam Witteveen, "Microsoft Joins the Local AI Push," 2026-10-08, 03:19–04:23
**Narration:** The next test is arriving now. Microsoft says its hybrid intelligence, powered by HydraFusion, comes to the GitHub Copilot app in experimental preview later in October. It sends some work to models running on your own machine. [03-c6] The YouTuber Sam Witteveen cites an October fifteenth ship date. [03-c6] He also asks the key question. If the local model can't handle a task, does it hand the task back to the cloud? Or do you just get a worse answer and never know what failed? Microsoft, he notes, didn't show that. [03-c6]

## Scene 7: Show the trail, or hide it?
**Visual:** Two-panel diagram. The left panel shows a chat bubble with the status line "researching pumpkin patches" and stacked layers beneath it revealed by taps: "steps," then "full log." The right panel shows a results card with a visible "Sources searched" list and a "Gaps" note.
**On-screen text:** Hidden behind taps, or shown up front?
**Source on screen:** How I AI, "How Meta designed Muse: an AI agent people actually want to talk to," 2026-10-12, 08:01–08:49; Nielsen Norman Group, "The New Siri's Biggest Strength Is Not Intelligence," 2026-10-09; The Vergecast, "The $8 million AI music scam," 2026-10-14, 03:38–04:19
**Narration:** Designers are split. On the How I AI podcast, Meta's design director for Muse describes a short status line by default. The full record of what the agent did sits a few taps deeper, there only if you need it. [03-c8] Nielsen Norman Group sets a firmer bar: an agentic experience must show which sources were searched and explain the gaps. [03-c8] Meanwhile, some services want no agents at all. The Verge reports that JPMorgan Chase CEO Jamie Dimon said the bank is blocking agents from doing banking tasks for customers. [03-c7]

## Scene 8: The rule, and what to watch
**Visual:** Checklist slide with three items, each with an empty checkbox: "Router admits local failures," "Sources shown up front," "More services blocking agents." A footer reads "Full article and sources in description."
**On-screen text:** Use agents where you can verify the result
**Source on screen:** none
**Narration:** Here's a practical rule. Use agents where you can verify the result, and treat silence about sources and routing as the main warning sign. [03-c3] [03-c8] Over the next few months, watch three things. Does Microsoft's router tell you when the local model falls short? Do assistants show their sources up front, or tuck them behind taps? [03-c8] And do more services block agents outright? [03-c7] These are signals, not predictions. The studies are small, and for low-stakes jobs like reminders, checking may matter less. The full article and all sources are in the description.
