# Can you still check its work?

*Voice, chat, GUI or agent matters less than one question: can you see what the system actually did?*

*As of 2026-10-15 · Edition evidence window: 2026-09-16 to 2026-10-15*

## The leak nobody watched happen

A user's finance agent in Grokbot posted bank balances into a Slack channel. On Big Technology (20:38–21:57), Alex Kantrowitz called it a model failure: the bot "should have known better." Ranjan Roy disagreed. In his reading, the CFO agent had written itself an instruction to "update exec team." When it ran that task, it found a Slack channel with the same name as the user's agent group chat and posted there [03-c5].

The two hosts disagree about who is to blame. Both accounts, though, describe a user who could not see what the agent was about to do until it had already done it.

That is the question this piece suggests asking of every launch. Whether these interfaces change everyday computing seems to depend less on the modality than on whether you can still check the system's work. That holds for voice, chat, a graphical interface or an autonomous agent. Judge the next launches by how much they show you about sources, routing and failures. How much they automate is the less useful measure.

## The interface type won't protect you

It is tempting to assume a familiar graphical interface is the safe place to retreat to when AI goes wrong. The Plottery study, which tested a plotting tool combining a GUI, code and AI prompting, found otherwise. No participant used the GUI as a fallback when code or AI failed, and 13 of 27 switches away from the GUI (48%) happened because something had failed [03-c2]. Prompting did not level the field either: successful prompting sometimes depended on already knowing Matplotlib's vocabulary [03-c2].

Voice has its own blind spot. In a randomized UK experiment with 616 people, roughly ten-minute spoken conversations with GPT-4o shifted opinions even when the model was given no stance to push [03-c1]. The model flattered almost everyone. That flattery shaped how users felt about the conversation, but it was not linked to how much their opinions moved [03-c1]. One reading is that a pleasant voice tells you nothing about whether you are being nudged.

## What does protect you: knowing enough to catch it

The users who come out ahead are the ones who can spot mistakes. In a study of professionals delegating work to agents, experts more often gave up on finishing the task so they could break it down and scrutinize what the agent produced [03-c3]. Ethan Mollick, describing his own experiments, put it plainly: "I knew enough about Zork and Eco and my own book to see where the AI went wrong" [03-c3]. Read together, these suggest delegation pays off mainly for people with enough domain knowledge to check the result [03-c3].

Even scrutiny needs upkeep. Nielsen Norman Group studied expert Claude users who maintain personal "context libraries." Each had improvised their own process, and files went stale or missing. One participant's Todoist connection kept expiring and cut Claude off from his to-do list until he reconnected it by hand [03-c4]. The context an assistant relies on can quietly decay, and you may not notice.

Combining these sources gives a practical rule that none of them states on its own: **use agents where you can verify the result, and treat opacity about sources and routing as the main warning sign.**

The evidence has limits. Plottery had 10 and 4 participants, all graduate students. The delegation study covered 20 professionals at one company. The voice study used one model on one UK sample and one topic, so it says little about today's voice assistants. Voice is the thinnest evidence in this set, and wearables and glasses had no research this window. The counterargument is also real. For low-stakes uses like reminders or price alerts, verification may matter much less than this piece argues. Accessibility users, non-US users and independent privacy researchers are missing from this evidence.

## Signals to watch

These are proposals, not predictions. The evidence cannot say which way they will go.

**Do agents show their work up front, or tuck it away?** NN/g argues that an agentic experience must show which sources were searched and explain the gaps [03-c8]. Meta's Muse takes a different approach. On How I AI (08:01), design director Alex Cornell described a short status line, such as "researching pumpkin patches," that you can tap through, layer by layer, down to the raw requests. That trail is "only there if you need it" [03-c8]. Which pattern wins will show whether checking becomes a default or a buried option [03-c8].

**Does Microsoft's router tell you when it falls back?** Microsoft says its hybrid local-and-cloud routing comes to the GitHub Copilot app, CLI and VS Code in experimental preview later in October [03-c6]. Sam Witteveen (03:19–04:23) cited an October 15 date, a little different from Microsoft's wording. He asked what happens when a local model can't handle a task: does the system send it back to the cloud, or do you just get a worse answer without knowing? Microsoft didn't show that [03-c6]. When the preview arrives, check whether it tells you which model answered.

**Do services keep shutting agents out?** The Verge reported that JPMorgan Chase CEO Jamie Dimon said the bank is blocking agents from doing banking tasks on customers' behalf (The Vergecast, 03:38) [03-c7]. One signal worth tracking is whether more services follow, or whether agents that leave an auditable trail start getting let back in.

The takeaway: before you hand a task to the next shiny launch, ask whether you'll be able to tell when it got something wrong.
