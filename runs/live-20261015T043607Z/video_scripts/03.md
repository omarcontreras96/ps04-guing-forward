# HAL gets the first word, not the last

*As of 2026-10-15*

## Scene 1: The ten-minute phone call
**Visual:** Illustrated phone screen showing an active voice call labeled "Dots," with a moving audio waveform. Off to one side, a blank text box with a blinking cursor fades out.
**On-screen text:** Ten minutes of talking instead of typing
**Source on screen:** The Vergecast, "How much should you tell your AI agent?", 2026-10-06, 15:47–16:20
**Narration:** A Verge reviewer, Allison Johnson, had a website redesign to describe. Instead of typing it out, she phoned OpenAI's agent, Dots, and talked for about ten minutes. [03-c3] She went section by section, checking, so you're with me? [03-c3] When she asked it to go do all that, it said, yep, got it. [03-c3] Her verdict was that it wasn't perfect, but it beat sitting and typing paragraphs of thoughts. [03-c3] Science fiction has imagined this since HAL in 2001: A Space Odyssey. Is talking really the best way to use AI, or just the most cinematic?

## Scene 2: Our answer up front
**Visual:** Simple two-column slide. The left column, labeled "Start and hand off," has a speech-bubble icon. The right column, labeled "Finish and check," has a checklist and slider icon.
**On-screen text:** Talk to start. Check with your eyes and hands.
**Source on screen:** none
**Narration:** Our read of this month's evidence is that talking to the computer is becoming the best way to start a task and hand it off. It is not the best way to finish one, or to check it. The interfaces worth adopting will show their sources and give you direct controls back. The most humanlike voice or face is not what decides it.

## Scene 3: Words need vocabulary
**Visual:** Mock chart-editing window with graphical controls on one side and an AI prompt box on the other. Arrows between them are labeled "switch." A bar diagram compares "nearly half" with "one in eight."
**On-screen text:** Leaving the GUI: often failure. Leaving AI: rarely.
**Source on screen:** arXiv cs.HC, "Plottery: A Code-Integrated GUI for Matplotlib", 2026-10-14
**Narration:** Take Plottery, a research tool for Matplotlib, a popular charting library. It offers graphical controls alongside AI prompting. Researchers watched ten participants move between the two. When people left the graphical controls, nearly half the time it was because something had failed. [03-c1] When they left the AI prompt, failure was the reason only about one time in eight. [03-c1] But prompting sometimes depended on knowing Matplotlib's own vocabulary. [03-c1] Our read is that plain words help most when you already know what to ask for.

## Scene 4: The voice that moves you
**Visual:** Slide with a speech waveform feeding into an opinion scale whose needle drifts downward. A small panel lists "616 people, about 10 minutes, spoken."
**On-screen text:** A voice with no stance still shifted opinions
**Source on screen:** arXiv cs.HC, "The AI's Poker Face: Contrasting Persuasion and Sycophancy in Voice-Based Multi-Turn Conversations", 2026-10-14
**Narration:** Now the risk. In a separate randomized study, six hundred and sixteen people had spoken conversations of about ten minutes with GPT-4o. [03-c2] Even when the model was given no stance, opinions still shifted. [03-c2] How much the model simply agreed with people, its sycophancy, was not linked to how much their opinions changed. [03-c2] Our read is that the warmth of a spoken exchange is not evidence that it is accurate. Keep the limits in mind, though. This was one model, one topic, a UK sample, and opinions measured right afterward.

## Scene 5: A face on the voice
**Visual:** Screenshot of the Gemini Live avatar picker on Google Cloud, showing pre-built cartoon-style avatars only. A timing bar shows the transcript arriving just ahead of the audio.
**On-screen text:** Responsive, slight lag, custom avatars still gated
**Source on screen:** Sam Witteveen, "Gemini Live Avatars", 2026-09-27, 02:42–03:23 and 05:02–05:43
**Narration:** Faces are arriving too. Sam Witteveen tested Google's Gemini Live avatars, which developers can build with on Google Cloud now, and found them pretty responsive. [03-c4] He also noted a delay, with the transcript arriving slightly before the voice. [03-c4] Custom avatars, made from your own photo and voice, are still behind an allow list and a verification process. [03-c4] A face adds presence. It does not add a way to check the work.

## Scene 6: The price of being known
**Visual:** Mock connectors page with toggles for email, calendar, health, financial and medical records. A second panel shows a to-do app icon with a broken-link symbol and a "reconnect" button.
**On-screen text:** More context, more upkeep
**Source on screen:** The Vergecast, "How much should you tell your AI agent?", 2026-10-06, 34:34–35:11; Nielsen Norman Group, "Context Libraries: How AI Power Users Manage Context Engineering", 2026-10-09
**Narration:** Handing off a task works best when the agent knows you, and that has a price. On The Vergecast, David Pierce described Muse's connectors page asking for email, calendar, health data, financial data and medical records. [03-c6] He said every connector he turned on made the product better, in a direct linear way. [03-c6] Keeping that context working is a chore of its own. Nielsen Norman Group studied expert Claude users, and one participant's Todoist connection kept expiring, which cut Claude off from his to-do list until he reconnected it by hand. [03-c5] Participants also rarely checked what Claude had changed in their files. [03-c5]

## Scene 7: What checking could look like
**Visual:** Three stacked phone mockups. The first shows a one-line status, "Researching pumpkin patches." The second shows a list of completed steps. The third shows a dense technical log.
**On-screen text:** Status line, then action list, then raw log
**Source on screen:** How I AI (Claire Vo), "How Meta designed Muse: an AI agent people actually want to talk to | Alex Cornell (Design Director)", 2026-10-12, 08:01–08:49
**Narration:** So what does checking look like? Alex Cornell, Meta's design director for Muse, describes one approach. [03-c7] By default, you see a short status line, such as "researching pumpkin patches." [03-c7] If you tap it, you get a list of everything the agent has done. [03-c7] If you tap further, you reach the raw technical detail, which is there for auditing but stays out of everyone else's way. [03-c7] That is Meta's design rationale, not evidence that it works.

## Scene 8: The takeaway and what to watch
**Visual:** Checklist slide reading "Talk to start / Look before you accept / One scoped source at a time." A second slide shows two signal icons: a "sources searched" panel and a Windows permission dialog.
**On-screen text:** Watch for shown sources and real permission controls
**Source on screen:** Nielsen Norman Group, "The New Siri's Biggest Strength Is Not Intelligence", 2026-10-09; Windows Blog, "Building Windows for hybrid intelligence", 2026-10-07
**Narration:** The takeaway is to talk to start, look before you accept, and grant access one scoped source at a time. Our read is that two signals would show checkability, not conversation, becoming the product. The first is assistants that tell you which sources they searched, as Nielsen Norman Group recommends. The second is Copilot's hybrid features actually reaching Copilot+ PCs in the coming months Microsoft has promised, with permission controls you can see. [03-c8] If neither happens, this reading needs another look. [03-c8] There is one gap. We found no usable evidence this month on smart glasses, on always-on wearables, or on accessibility users, for whom voice may be the main way in. ## YouTube description
Talking to AI is turning out to be a strong way to start a task and hand it off. This month's evidence suggests it is a weak way to check the result. We look at a charting-tool study, a voice-persuasion experiment, hands-on reports on Dots, Muse and Gemini Live avatars, and Nielsen Norman Group research on the upkeep personal context demands. Then we name the signals that would confirm or overturn this reading.
