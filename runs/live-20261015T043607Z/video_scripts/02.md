# AI Agents Can Act. Can You Check Them?

*As of 2026-10-15*

## Scene 1: The wrong doctor

**Visual:** A simple illustrated list of three doctor names, each with a phone number beneath it. An animated arrow shows the numbers sliding one row out of place. No faces.
**On-screen text:** Right numbers. Wrong doctors.
**Source on screen:** Big Technology Podcast, "Meta & Microsoft's Claude Slowdown, His Agent Leaked His Banking Info, Don't Bully Your AI," 2026-10-09, 14:07–14:40
**Narration:** Alex Kantrowitz's wife thought she had booked an appointment with one doctor. It turned out she had booked a completely different one. [02-c5] Kantrowitz said on the Big Technology Podcast that his Muse agent had listed working phone numbers under the wrong doctors' names. [02-c5] Nothing about it was dramatic. It was just quietly wrong. So here's our question. Which claims about agents acting for us survive independent testing? And where do vendor numbers and real experience pull apart?

## Scene 2: What holds up

**Visual:** A diagram of a billing ticket in a generic admin app. Three flags are marked on it: "Wrong account," "Duplicate refund," and "Planted prompt injection." Each flag turns to a green check in sequence.
**On-screen text:** Agents can finish real multi-step tasks
**Source on screen:** How I AI, "Meta's new 'Muse Wonder' model: good for AI agents, bad for design," 2026-10-14, 11:53–13:12
**Narration:** Start with what holds up. Claire Vo, who hosts How I AI, runs her own computer-use test. An agent has to resolve a billing ticket inside an admin app. The ticket is linked to the wrong account. There's a duplicate refund. And there's a prompt injection planted in the instructions. In her testing, Meta's new Muse Wonder model fixed the account link, issued the correct refund, avoided the planted trap, and did it better than every other model she tried. [02-c4] So yes, agents can complete real multi-step tasks.

## Scene 3: The vendor's number

**Visual:** A slide with a large "0.7%" beside a shield icon, labeled "Self-reported by Google." A small gray box reads "Independent check: none found."
**On-screen text:** Google: 0.7% prompt-injection success, self-reported
**Source on screen:** Google Keyword, "Developing Gemini 4 Argon responsibly and safely," 2026-10-13
**Narration:** Vendors are making safety claims, too. Google says its Gemini four Argon model cut attack success on Gray Swan's indirect prompt-injection benchmark to zero point seven percent, across fifteen adaptive attempts. [02-c1] That's Google reporting on its own model, and nothing in our evidence checks it independently. [02-c1] So we neither confirm nor refute it. Notice what it measures, though. Whether the model resists an attacker.

## Scene 4: Errors nobody noticed

**Visual:** A grid of fifteen notebook icons. Eleven turn red with a small "label leakage" tag. A row of eye icons beside them stays closed. A second panel shows a "Trust" bar linked by lines to "Confidence" and "Satisfaction" but not to "Quality."
**On-screen text:** 11 of 15 flawed. Zero noticed.
**Source on screen:** arXiv cs.HC, "Impact of Expertise and Trust on AI-assisted Work," 2026-10-14
**Narration:** Independent evidence points somewhere else. A new think-aloud study on arXiv watched professionals build machine learning models with GitHub Copilot's agent mode. Eleven of the fifteen notebooks with trained models contained a flaw called label leakage. [02-c2] None of their authors noticed, experts or novices. [02-c2] And trust in the AI tracked people's confidence and satisfaction. [02-c3] It did not track the quality of their work. [02-c3]

## Scene 5: Lost in the handoff

**Visual:** A two-step diagram. Step one is a chat bubble reading "Start from brother's address ✓." Step two is a generic map pin labeled "Current location." A broken arrow connects them.
**On-screen text:** Confirmed in step one, lost in step two
**Source on screen:** Nielsen Norman Group, "The New Siri's Biggest Strength Is Not Intelligence," 2026-10-09
**Narration:** Nielsen Norman Group saw a similar slip with Apple's new Siri. In their test, Siri AI confirmed that directions should start from the tester's brother's address. [02-c6] Then it opened Google Maps with a route from the tester's current location instead. [02-c6] The confirmed detail simply didn't make it into the action. [02-c6]

## Scene 6: The batting average

**Visual:** A baseball-style scoreboard reading "Useful: 50–60%" and "Not useful: 40–50%." A smaller line below reads "Better with a good example."
**On-screen text:** Copilot briefings: useful about half the time
**Source on screen:** Nate B Jones, "OpenClaw Was Too Dangerous To Install. 7 Months Later Microsoft Is Bringing It To Your Company.," YouTube, 2026-10-02, 05:45–06:15
**Narration:** Practitioners describe something similar day to day. Nate B Jones, who has used Microsoft's Copilot, calls its briefings a batting average, useful about fifty to sixty percent of the time. [02-c7] He says that goes up if you show it a good previous briefing as an example. [02-c7] That can still save time, but only if someone checks.

## Scene 7: What the numbers don't measure

**Visual:** A split screen. The left side is labeled "What vendor figures measure" and shows a shield blocking an attacker icon. The right side is labeled "What went wrong in independent tests" and shows a person icon looking past a small error flag. Underneath are three gray boxes marked "Searched," "Carried over," and "Assumed," each with a closed lid.
**On-screen text:** Resisting attackers is not the same as being checkable
**Source on screen:** none
**Narration:** Put these sources together and a pattern appears that none of them states alone. The agent usually does something plausible. The person doesn't catch the error, because the interface hides what was searched, carried over, or assumed. Our read is that Google's figure measures whether the model resists attackers, while the failures in independent tests were mostly errors people didn't notice, so vendor safety numbers are not evidence that you can trust an agent without checking. [02-c8]

## Scene 8: A fair caveat

**Visual:** A plain slide with a short checklist: "Small study," "Requests run once or twice," "Self-designed test," "Single anecdote." Beside it sit two upward arrows labeled "Agents more capable" and "Checking still needed."
**On-screen text:** Small evidence, and two trends at once
**Source on screen:** none
**Narration:** A fair caveat. Most of this independent evidence is small or informal. The study had twenty participants from one company, working on one task. Nielsen Norman Group ran most requests once or twice. Vo designed her own test. The doctor story is one anecdote. And Vo's prompt-injection result suggests agents are getting more reliable. [02-c4] The evidence supports both trends. It doesn't show that reliability removes the need to check.

## Scene 9: Where we land

**Visual:** A closing slide with three numbered watch items and the GUIng Forward wordmark. An arrow points down toward the description.
**On-screen text:** Agents can act. Checking is still your job.
**Source on screen:** none
**Narration:** So here's where we land. Agents can act. Checking them is still your job, and today's interfaces make that hard. Watch for three things. First, an independent check of Google's zero point seven percent figure. Second, tests that measure whether people catch an agent's mistakes, not just whether models resist attacks. Third, interfaces that show what an agent searched, carried over, and assumed. The full article and every source are linked in the description.

## YouTube description

AI agents can now complete real multi-step tasks, and one independent test shows a model working through a planted prompt injection. But the failures in this month's independent evidence were mostly errors people didn't notice: flawed models no author caught, a confirmed detail lost in a handoff, and phone numbers under the wrong doctors. Vendor safety figures measure whether models resist attackers, not whether you can check what an agent did.
