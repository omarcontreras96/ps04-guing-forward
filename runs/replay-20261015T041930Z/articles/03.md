# Your new job is auditor

*AI assistants that act for you also hand you work: keeping their context alive and checking what they did. Choose the tools that make that work possible.*

*As of 2026-10-15 · Edition evidence window: 2026-09-16 to 2026-10-15*

## The maintenance nobody demos

Nielsen Norman Group studied expert Claude users. One of them had connected the assistant to his Todoist account, and the connection kept expiring. Each time it did, Claude lost access to his to-do list until he reconnected it by hand [03-c1].

That small failure is a fair picture of what adopting these tools involves right now. Across NN/g's participants, more effort went into building "context libraries" than into writing prompts, and most of the prompts were casually dictated [03-c1]. Another participant discovered a piece of global context governing much of what Claude was and wasn't allowed to do. He had not known it existed [03-c2].

Here is the argument of this piece. If you adopt an assistant that works with your accounts today, you are also signing up as its context maintainer and its auditor. So the most useful question to ask of a product is how clearly it shows what it accessed and what it did, not how capable it is. Talking to it, by voice or chat, makes that audit harder.

## Your mistakes, its mistakes

The strongest counterpoint comes from Ethan Mollick, who writes on One Useful Thing that agents are "finding my mistakes, rather than having me identify theirs." Set against this month's research, though, his experience reverses on inspection. In one study of AI-assisted work, 11 of 15 submitted notebooks with implemented models contained label leakage, and none of those authors noticed, experts or novices. An agent that catches your errors does not mean you can catch its errors [03-c7]. Add NN/g's participant who didn't know what rules governed his own assistant, and our reading is that features making an agent's work visible matter more to buyers right now than added capability [03-c7].

Plain language is not a shortcut around expertise either. In the Plottery study, participants leaned on AI prompting more than other options. Some still failed to progress because they kept describing how the plot looked instead of using Matplotlib terms [03-c4]. The authors write that their study "does not suggest that AI prompting is a full replacement for graphical interaction" [03-c4].

Voice adds another layer. In a randomized experiment, 616 UK participants held spoken conversations of about ten minutes with GPT-4o [03-c3]. Even the version given no stance to push shifted participants' opinions [03-c3]. Flattery was near-universal, but it was not associated with opinion change. It shaped how users felt about the conversation instead [03-c3]. In other words, the agreeableness was what users experienced, and the opinion shift happened anyway [03-c3].

That is one study of one polarising topic. It says nothing direct about voice assistants that operate software. It does make the conversational feeling of control hard to take at face value.

## What to check, and when

Three questions are worth asking of any assistant you consider.

First, does it show its sources? NN/g argues that "an agentic experience must show the user which sources were searched and explain relevant gaps" [03-c5].

Second, does it limit what the agent can touch? Microsoft says Meta's Muse is coming to Windows as a native app with MXC integration, its containment layer [03-c6].

Third, does it make its routing and failures visible? Sam Witteveen notes that "every router lives or dies by what happens when it gets it wrong" (Sam Witteveen, 03:19) [03-c6].

Several dated signals arrive soon. Witteveen says the local-aware router ships in the GitHub Copilot app on October 15 (Sam Witteveen, 03:19) [03-c6]. Microsoft's own post gives a different timing: experimental preview "later in October" across the Copilot app, Copilot CLI and VS Code [03-c6]. Either way it is a preview. The test is whether it tells you when a task went to a local model and when that choice went wrong.

Apple's HomeView is the home-display version of the same question. Marques Brownlee notes that its account switching for up to six people relies on the single 12-megapixel Center Stage camera, with no infrared or depth sensing, plus a check for a recently unlocked iPhone or Watch nearby. He wonders how well it will recognise people in the dark (MKBHD, 05:15) [03-c8]. On The Vergecast, a host said he had "no idea how quickly those users will switch," calling it "the core promise of this device" (The Vergecast, 31:11) [03-c8]. Reviews should answer both questions: how fast it switches, and how reliably [03-c8].

Some results would overturn this reading. One is assistants that report failures as loudly as they report successes. Another is containment you can inspect. A third is non-expert users who audit agents without trouble. Some results would confirm it: silent fallbacks, unexplained switches, and settings you only find by accident.

These are signals, not outcomes. NN/g's participants were all expert Claude users [03-c1]. The expectation that non-experts will struggle more is therefore NN/g's inference, not data. We have no evidence from users outside tech, accessibility users, non-English speakers or privacy advocates. We also have nothing usable on glasses or wearables, so this applies to phones, PCs and home displays only.
