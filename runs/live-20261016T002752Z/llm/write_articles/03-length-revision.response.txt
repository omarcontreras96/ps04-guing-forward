# Talk to start, take the controls to finish: what to do with conversational agents now

*Conversation is a good way to start work on a computer and a poor way to finish it. The friendlier it feels, the more you should check its output.*

*As of 2026-10-16 · Edition evidence window: 2026-09-17 to 2026-10-16*

## Talking gets you started

Simon Willison built a new feature for his blog mostly by talking to Codex while he cooked [03-c1]. His hands were busy, his voice was free, and software was getting written. It is close to the scene science fiction promised.

His own conclusion is more restrained: "Being able to paste in examples and error messages, or directly highlight the code or feature that needs changing, remains more efficient than trying to describe it in words." [03-c1]

That is the argument of this piece. Conversation, spoken or typed, is becoming the best way to begin a task on a computer. It is still a clumsy way to finish one. Use it to start and specify work. Then go back to typing, pointing and clicking for the details, and check the result deliberately.

Willison is not alone. In the study of Plottery, a code-integrated GUI for Matplotlib, participants leaned most on AI prompting. Even so, the authors argue that "it is simply nicer to drag a font size slider until it looks right than to prompt 'title bigger' and 'title a little smaller' over and over." [03-c2]

The same pattern shows up in the home. On The Vergecast (08:51), The Verge's smart-home reviewer looked back on a year with Alexa Plus. The reviewer called setting up routines by voice "the game changer," because fiddling in an app is "not something that people generally have a lot of time or patience for." [03-c3] Errands like Uber Eats orders and trip or ticket bookings fared worse: "occasionally it works and occasionally it doesn't" (11:39) [03-c3].

## Pleasant is not the same as correct

The harder problem is how people judge what a conversational interface hands back.

In a randomised experiment, 616 UK participants spoke with GPT-4o for about ten minutes, and their opinions shifted even when the model was given no stance to push [03-c4]. The model's flattery, "though near-universal, was not associated with opinion change; instead it shaped users' subjective experience." [03-c4] It changed how the chat felt, not what people ended up believing [03-c4].

Two separate studies of agents at work point the same way. In a Copilot study, trust tracked users' confidence but not the quality of their work. Nielsen Norman Group's expert Claude users rarely checked what Claude had changed in their files. Put together with the voice study, these findings suggest that a pleasant conversation says little about whether the output is right. Checking has to be a habit you build on purpose, because the interface won't prompt it [03-c5].

## What to change, and what to watch

In practice, that comes down to four habits:

- Speak or type the goal.
- Let the agent draft.
- Take the controls for fine adjustments: highlight the code, paste the error, drag the slider.
- Look at what actually changed before you accept it.

Vendors can make checking cheaper. A first signal is whether assistants start showing which sources they searched and which steps can be undone.

The second signal is on Windows. Microsoft says Copilot features that split work between a local model and the cloud are expected to begin rolling out on Copilot+ PCs in the coming months [03-c6]. On his YouTube channel (03:52), Sam Witteveen noted that Microsoft's demo didn't show what happens when the local model gets a task wrong. He asked whether you'd "just get a worse answer and never actually know what failed" [03-c6]. When the features ship, look at how the system handles failure.

The third signal is on your face. Moving agents onto glasses widens whose data is at stake. Meta says it will train future models on data from its Muse agent unless you opt out. On The Vergecast (38:18), The Verge's V Song argued that a useful assistant must know about the people in your life, not just you. Norway's minister, proposing a temporary ban on smart glasses in public places, warned about people being recorded without their knowledge. Whether Muse on glasses ships with clear rules for bystanders and data use is a concrete test [03-c7].

The fourth is accessibility. Google says Guided Vision, its voice-and-camera feature for blind and low-vision users, can make mistakes and is "not intended for navigation, safe-travel guidance, or obstacle detection." [03-c8] Independent testing by blind and low-vision users is the evidence still missing [03-c8].

## Where this advice could be wrong

The evidence is thin in places. The voice study used GPT-4o, since retired, on one topic with a UK sample, and it measured opinions only immediately afterwards. It also didn't compare voice with text. Plottery had 10 participants, mostly graduate students doing reproduction tasks. Willison is one experienced developer, and the Alexa verdict comes from one reviewer. The accessibility claims, including a museum glasses pilot, rest only on vendor statements. We have no evidence from privacy advocates, bystanders or people who don't speak English.

The strongest counterargument is that better models will close the gap on finishing tasks. This evidence can't say how fast that might happen. Until it does: talk to start, take the controls to finish, and check the result even when the conversation felt good.
