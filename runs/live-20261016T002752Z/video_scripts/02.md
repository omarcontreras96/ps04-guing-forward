# AI Agents Ace the Test, Then Fail Where You Can't See

*As of 2026-10-16*

## Scene 1: Did Muse just buy me a car?
**Visual:** Slide showing a chat bubble that reads "Do you want me to buy this one?" Below it, a reply "yes" sits under an older message, and a loading spinner appears.
**On-screen text:** "Did Muse just buy me a car?"
**Source on screen:** The Vergecast, "How much should you tell your AI agent?", 2026-10-06, 32:59
**Narration:** On the Vergecast, David Pierce described using Meta's Muse to find car listings. It asked him if he wanted it to buy one. He replied yes, but to an earlier message, and the screen started loading. [02-c2] It didn't buy the car. The process stopped. [02-c2] So which claims about AI agents hold up when people test them? And where do the promises and the behavior split apart?

## Scene 2: The promise
**Visual:** Screenshot of Meta's Muse Wonder announcement page, with the phrase "Active Focus" highlighted.
**On-screen text:** Meta's claim: checks in before anything important
**Source on screen:** Meta Newsroom, "Introducing Muse Wonder and Muse Wonder Full", 2026-10-12
**Narration:** Start with what Meta says. According to Meta, its new Muse Wonder models have a feature called Active Focus. It includes asking for clarification when needed, and checking in before following through on anything important. [02-c1] That is a claim about safe autonomy. A demo can show it, but only everyday use can prove it.

## Scene 3: Where it holds
**Visual:** Simple diagram of a billing ticket with three warning icons labeled "wrong account," "duplicate refund," and "prompt injection," each turning into a green check.
**On-screen text:** Three planted traps. All three caught.
**Source on screen:** How I AI, "Meta's new 'Muse Wonder' model", 2026-10-14, 11:53
**Narration:** On a well-defined task, Meta's claims look good. Claire Vo, host of How I AI, gave agents a computer-use test: resolve a billing ticket in an admin app. The ticket was linked to the wrong account. There was a duplicate refund. And a prompt injection was hidden in the instructions. [02-c7] By her account, Muse Wonder fixed the account, issued the correct refund and handled every trap. She ranked it best of the models she tested. [02-c7] One caveat: this is her own benchmark, and nobody can reproduce it from what she has published.

## Scene 4: Where it slips
**Visual:** Mock contact list with three doctor names, where the phone-number lines shift down one row under the wrong names.
**On-screen text:** Real numbers, wrong names
**Source on screen:** Big Technology Podcast, "Meta & Microsoft's Claude Slowdown…", 2026-10-09, 14:07
**Narration:** Everyday tasks are messier. On the Vergecast, Allison Johnson said Muse completes a purchase once you type "go ahead." There's no button to click. [02-c2] On Big Technology, Alex Kantrowitz said Muse put working phone numbers under the wrong doctors' names. His wife called one and booked with a different doctor than she meant to. [02-c3] These are single incidents, not failure rates. Still, notice that nothing looked broken while it was happening.

## Scene 5: Version and task
**Visual:** Timeline slide with three markers: Vergecast on October 6, Big Technology on October 9, Muse Wonder announced on October 12.
**On-screen text:** Different version, different task
**Source on screen:** none
**Narration:** So who's right? Our read is that the sources don't really contradict each other. Both podcast episodes aired before Meta announced Wonder, so they likely describe an earlier version of Muse doing messier work. We can't confirm which version those testers used. Task and version explain the gap better than declaring a winner.

## Scene 6: The research
**Visual:** Bar graphic showing fifteen notebooks, eleven shaded red, with a caption reading "noticed by authors: zero."
**On-screen text:** 11 of 15 leaked. 0 noticed.
**Source on screen:** arXiv, "Impact of Expertise and Trust on AI-assisted Work", 2026-10-14
**Narration:** The research shows a pattern that no single anecdote can. In a study of twenty professionals using GitHub Copilot's agent mode, eleven of the fifteen notebooks that trained a model contained label leakage, and none of their authors noticed. [02-c5] Microsoft funded that study. Everyone came from one company, and the authors call the results hypothesis-generating. In a preprint called TaskVM, standard computer-use agents often failed in two ways. They claimed a task was done too early, or they changed things they should have left alone. [02-c6]

## Scene 7: Confirmed, then ignored
**Visual:** Phone mockup with Siri confirming a typed starting address, then Google Maps opening with "Current location" as the start point.
**On-screen text:** You confirmed it. It ignored it.
**Source on screen:** Nielsen Norman Group, "The New Siri's Biggest Strength Is Not Intelligence", 2026-10-09
**Narration:** Nielsen Norman Group found a small example of the same thing. In their hands-on test, Siri asked the tester to confirm a starting address for directions. Then it opened Google Maps with a route from his current location instead. [02-c4] Our read is that this is the common thread: agent failures look like success.

## Scene 8: Talking to it
**Visual:** Screenshot of Google's Live Avatar interface beside a split timeline where the transcript bar starts slightly ahead of the audio bar.
**On-screen text:** "Fluid turn-taking": partly true
**Source on screen:** Sam Witteveen, "Gemini Live Avatars", YouTube, 2026-09-27, 05:02
**Narration:** Voice shows a smaller version of the gap. Google says its Live Avatar has fluid turn-taking. In Sam Witteveen's hands-on demo, he found it pretty responsive. There was still a delay, and the text transcript arrived slightly before the voice. So Google's claim holds, but only in part. [02-c8]

## Scene 9: What to take away
**Visual:** Closing slide with two columns. The left column reads "Bounded task, clean data: holds up." The right column reads "Safe autonomy: check the work."
**On-screen text:** Check the work, especially when it looks finished
**Source on screen:** none
**Narration:** Here's the takeaway. Agents hold up on bounded tasks with clean data. They break down where vendors promise safe autonomy, like checking in first or knowing when a job is done. There, independent tests show quiet failures that users miss. So check the work, especially when it looks finished. Next, watch for independent tests of Muse Wonder on ordinary, messy errands, and see whether it really checks in before it spends money. The full article and every source are in the description.
